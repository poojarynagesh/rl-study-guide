Learn Policy Gradient

Learn the policy itself instead of values: parameterisations (softmax, Gaussian), the policy gradient theorem derived step by step, REINFORCE, baselines, actor–critic, with pictures and fully worked numerical updates. Book: Sutton & Barto Ch 13.

Full chapter 13 → ← Learn Function Approximation Learn DQN →
One-paragraph summary

Value-based methods learn $q(s,a)$ and derive a policy by $\arg\max$. Policy-gradient methods learn a parameterised policy $\pi(a|s,\boldsymbol\theta)$ directly and improve $\boldsymbol\theta$ by gradient ascent on the expected return $J(\boldsymbol\theta)$. The policy gradient theorem says $\nabla J\propto\mathbb E_\pi\big[q_\pi(S,A)\,\nabla\ln\pi(A|S,\boldsymbol\theta)\big]$ — so we need no derivative of the unknown state distribution. Replace $q_\pi$ by the sampled return (REINFORCE), subtract a baseline to cut variance, or bootstrap with a learned critic (actor–critic).

1. Value-based vs policy-based

Advantage of parameterising the policyWhy
Smooth changeAction probabilities change continuously with $\boldsymbol\theta$ — stronger convergence guarantees than ε-greedy on $\hat q$, where a tiny change can swap the greedy action.
Can learn stochastic optimal policiesE.g. a game of rock–paper–scissors or aliased states (short-corridor example): the best policy is a specific mix, which ε-greedy cannot represent.
Continuous / huge action spacesNo $\arg\max_a$ over infinitely many actions; the policy outputs a Gaussian over actions.
Prior knowledgeThe policy form can encode what a good controller looks like.
Simpler function may sufficeThe policy can be simpler than the value function.
Disadvantages

Typically converges to a local optimum; evaluating a policy is high-variance and sample-hungry; learning is slower than value-based methods on small problems.

2. Parameterising the policy

2.1 Discrete actions: softmax in action preferences

$$\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)=\frac{e^{h(s,a,\boldsymbol\theta)}}{\sum_{b}e^{h(s,b,\boldsymbol\theta)}},\qquad h(s,a,\boldsymbol\theta)=\boldsymbol\theta^{\!\top}\mathbf x(s,a)$$
Softmax policy(book eq. 13.2–13.3)
$$\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\ln\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathbf x(s,a)-\sum_{b}\pi(b\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\,\mathbf x(s,b)$$
Score function (eligibility vector), softmax-linear(book Ex. 13.3)

Interpretation: “the features of the action taken” minus “the average features under the current policy”. Taking a more probable-than-average action moves the gradient little; taking a rare action moves it a lot.

2.2 Continuous actions: Gaussian policy

$$\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)=\frac{1}{\sigma(s,\boldsymbol\theta)\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(a-\mu(s,\boldsymbol\theta))^2}{2\,\sigma(s,\boldsymbol\theta)^2}\Big),\quad \mu=\boldsymbol\theta_\mu^{\!\top}\mathbf x_\mu(s),\ \ \sigma=\exp\!\big(\boldsymbol\theta_\sigma^{\!\top}\mathbf x_\sigma(s)\big)$$
Gaussian policy(book eq. 13.19–13.20)
$$\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta_\mu}\ln\pi=\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma^2}\,\mathbf x_\mu(s),\qquad \nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta_\sigma}\ln\pi=\Big(\frac{(a-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2}-1\Big)\mathbf x_\sigma(s)$$
Gaussian score function(book Ex. 13.4)

The exponential keeps $\sigma>0$. If a sampled action far from the mean ($|a-\mu|>\sigma$) earns above-baseline return, the mean moves toward it and $\sigma$ grows; if it lies within one $\sigma$, $\sigma$ shrinks (exploit).

3. The objective: expected return as a function of the policy parameters

For an episodic task starting in $s_0$:

$$J(\boldsymbol\theta)\;\doteq\;v_{\pi_{\boldsymbol\theta}}(s_0)$$
Performance measure (episodic)(book eq. 13.4)

We want $\boldsymbol\theta_{t+1}=\boldsymbol\theta_t+\alpha\,\widehat{\nabla J(\boldsymbol\theta_t)}$ — gradient ascent. The difficulty: $J$ depends on $\boldsymbol\theta$ both through the actions chosen and through the state distribution $\mu_\pi(s)$ the policy induces, and the latter depends on the (unknown) environment dynamics. The theorem removes the awkward term.

4. The policy gradient theorem (step-by-step derivation)

$$\nabla J(\boldsymbol\theta)\;\propto\;\sum_{s}\mu(s)\sum_{a}q_\pi(s,a)\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)$$
Policy gradient theorem(book eq. 13.5)

Sketch of the proof (episodic case, $\nabla$ means $\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}$, undiscounted for clarity):

  1. Start from the Bellman identity $v_\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a|s)\,q_\pi(s,a)$ and differentiate by the product rule: $$\nabla v_\pi(s)=\sum_a\Big[\nabla\pi(a|s)\,q_\pi(s,a)+\pi(a|s)\,\nabla q_\pi(s,a)\Big]$$
  2. Expand $q_\pi(s,a)=\sum_{s',r}p(s',r|s,a)\,(r+v_\pi(s'))$; only $v_\pi(s')$ depends on $\boldsymbol\theta$, so $\nabla q_\pi(s,a)=\sum_{s'}p(s'|s,a)\nabla v_\pi(s')$.
  3. Substitute: $\nabla v_\pi(s)=\sum_a\nabla\pi(a|s)q_\pi(s,a)+\sum_a\pi(a|s)\sum_{s'}p(s'|s,a)\nabla v_\pi(s')$. The right-hand side contains $\nabla v_\pi$ of the next state: unroll it repeatedly.
  4. After unrolling, $\nabla v_\pi(s_0)=\sum_{x}\Pr(s_0\to x)\sum_a\nabla\pi(a|x)\,q_\pi(x,a)$ where $\Pr(s_0\to x)$ is the probability of reaching $x$ under $\pi$.
  5. Summing the reach probabilities over time gives the on-policy visitation weight $\mu(x)$ (up to a constant, the mean episode length). Hence $\nabla J\propto\sum_x\mu(x)\sum_a q_\pi(x,a)\nabla\pi(a|x)$. $\blacksquare$
The punchline

$\nabla\mu$ never appears. We can estimate the gradient from samples generated by $\pi$ itself — without knowing the environment's dynamics.

The log-derivative trick: from a sum to an expectation

  1. Multiply and divide by $\pi$: $\sum_a q_\pi(s,a)\nabla\pi(a|s)=\sum_a\pi(a|s)\,q_\pi(s,a)\,\dfrac{\nabla\pi(a|s)}{\pi(a|s)}$.
  2. Recognise $\dfrac{\nabla\pi}{\pi}=\nabla\ln\pi$ and that $\sum_a\pi(a|s)(\cdot)=\mathbb E_{A\sim\pi}[\cdot]$.
  3. With $S$ drawn from $\mu$ too, we obtain an expectation over what the agent actually experiences:
$$\nabla J(\boldsymbol\theta)\;\propto\;\mathbb E_\pi\Big[q_\pi(S_t,A_t)\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\ln\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta)\Big]$$
Gradient as an expectation(book eq. 13.6)
Exam tip

Two derivations are examined repeatedly: (i) the log-derivative trick above, and (ii) the baseline-has-zero-mean proof in §6. Be able to write them in three lines.

5. REINFORCE: Monte Carlo policy gradient

  1. Replace $q_\pi(S_t,A_t)$ by its unbiased sample $G_t$ (since $\mathbb E_\pi[G_t|S_t,A_t]=q_\pi(S_t,A_t)$).
  2. Replace the expectation by the single sample actually seen at time $t$.
  3. Gradient ascent step, with the discount factor kept so that early steps count more:
$$\boldsymbol\theta_{t+1}=\boldsymbol\theta_t+\alpha\,\gamma^{t}\,G_t\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\ln\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)$$
REINFORCE update(book eq. 13.8)

Intuition. $\nabla\ln\pi(A_t|S_t)/1$ is the direction in parameter space that makes the taken action more likely. Multiplying by $G_t$ makes that move proportional to how good the outcome was: good returns ⇒ push the action up; bad (negative) returns ⇒ push it down.

REINFORCE: Monte-Carlo policy-gradient control (episodic) for π*on-policyno bootstrappingno model
Input: differentiable policy π(a|s,θ)
Parameter: step size α > 0
Initialise θ ∈ ℝᵈ arbitrarily
Loop forever (for each episode):
    Generate S₀,A₀,R₁,…,S_{T−1},A_{T−1},R_T following π(·|·,θ)
    Loop for t = 0,1,…,T−1:
        G ← Σ_{k=t+1..T} γ^{k−t−1} R_k            // return from step t
        θ ← θ + α · γᵗ · G · ∇ ln π(A_t|S_t,θ)
Unbiased but high variance; learns slowly; can update only after the episode ends.

6. REINFORCE with a baseline

Raw returns can be all positive (e.g. $G\in[90,110]$): every sampled action is pushed up, and learning works only through the small differences — noisy. Subtract a state-dependent baseline $b(s)$:

$$\nabla J\;\propto\;\sum_s\mu(s)\sum_a\big(q_\pi(s,a)-b(s)\big)\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)$$
Policy gradient with baseline(book eq. 13.10)

Why the answer is unchanged — the baseline term vanishes because $b(s)$ does not depend on $a$:

$$\sum_a b(s)\,\nabla\pi(a|s,\boldsymbol\theta)=b(s)\,\nabla\!\sum_a\pi(a|s,\boldsymbol\theta)=b(s)\,\nabla 1=0$$
Zero-mean baseline proof(book §13.4)
$$\boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta+\alpha\,\gamma^t\big(G_t-\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)\big)\nabla\ln\pi(A_t|S_t,\boldsymbol\theta),\qquad \mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w+\alpha^{\mathbf w}\gamma^t\big(G_t-\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)\big)\nabla\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)$$
REINFORCE with learned baseline $\hat v$(book p. 330)
Best baseline

A natural choice is $b(s)=\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)$ — then $G_t-\hat v$ estimates the advantage $q_\pi(s,a)-v_\pi(s)$. A baseline may depend on the state, but never on the action (otherwise the zero-mean proof fails).

7. Actor–critic methods

REINFORCE with a baseline still waits for the end of the episode ($G_t$) and is not truly online. Actor–critic replaces $G_t$ by a one-step bootstrapped target from the critic $\hat v(\cdot,\mathbf w)$, trading a little bias for much lower variance and fully incremental updates.

$$\delta_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma\,\hat v(S_{t+1},\mathbf w)-\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)$$
TD error (critic)(book eq. 13.12)
$$\mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w+\beta\,\delta\,\nabla\hat v(S,\mathbf w),\qquad \boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta+\alpha\,I\,\delta\,\nabla\ln\pi(A|S,\boldsymbol\theta),\qquad I\leftarrow\gamma I$$
One-step actor–critic updates(book p. 332)
One-step actor–critic (episodic), estimating π_θ ≈ π*on-policybootstrapsonline
Input: policy π(a|s,θ), state-value function v̂(s,w)
Parameters: step sizes α (actor), β (critic) > 0
Initialise θ, w
Loop forever (for each episode):
    Initialise S;  I ← 1
    Loop while S is not terminal:
        A ~ π(·|S,θ)
        Take A, observe S′, R
        δ ← R + γ·v̂(S′,w) − v̂(S,w)           // v̂(terminal)=0
        w ← w + β·δ·∇v̂(S,w)                    // critic: TD(0)
        θ ← θ + α·I·δ·∇ ln π(A|S,θ)            // actor: policy gradient with δ as advantage
        I ← γ·I;  S ← S′
Online and incremental; lower variance than REINFORCE; biased because the critic is approximate.
FactDetail
Two time scalesThe critic should learn faster than the actor: $\alpha\ll\beta$ (the actor changes the policy slowly so the critic can track $v_\pi$).
Why $\delta$ works$\mathbb E[\delta_t|S_t,A_t]=q_\pi(S_t,A_t)-v_\pi(S_t)$ when $\hat v=v_\pi$, i.e. the advantage.
Why the factor $I=\gamma^t$Matches the REINFORCE discounting; for $\gamma=1$ it equals 1 and disappears.
Continuing tasksUse average reward $r(\pi)$ and differential returns: $\delta=R-\bar R+\hat v(S')-\hat v(S)$, and update $\bar R\leftarrow\bar R+\alpha^{\bar R}\delta$.
BiasA wrong critic gives a biased actor update; that is the price for lower variance.

8. Worked numerical examples

Example A — softmax REINFORCE, one step

Problem

Two actions, tabular preferences $\boldsymbol\theta=(h_1,h_2)=(0,0)$, features one-hot: $\mathbf x(a_i)=\mathbf e_i$. The episode took $a_1$ at $t=0$ and the return was $G_0=2$. $\gamma=1$, $\alpha=0.1$. Compute the update and the new probability of $a_1$.

  1. Current policy: $\pi=\big(\tfrac{e^0}{e^0+e^0},\tfrac{e^0}{e^0+e^0}\big)=(0.5,\,0.5)$.
  2. Score function: $\nabla\ln\pi(a_1)=\mathbf x(a_1)-\sum_b\pi(b)\mathbf x(b)=(1,0)-(0.5,0.5)=(0.5,\,-0.5)$.
  3. Update: $\boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow(0,0)+0.1\cdot1\cdot2\cdot(0.5,-0.5)=(0.1,\,-0.1)$.
  4. New probability: $\pi(a_1)=\dfrac{e^{0.1}}{e^{0.1}+e^{-0.1}}=\dfrac{1.1052}{1.1052+0.9048}\approx\mathbf{0.550}$ (from 0.5). Rewarding $a_1$ made it more likely.
  5. Same state, return $G_0=-2$? Then $\boldsymbol\theta=(-0.1,0.1)$ and $\pi(a_1)\approx0.450$: bad outcome ⇒ less likely.

Example B — Gaussian policy score function

Problem

$\mu(s)=\theta_\mu x$ with $x=1$ and $\theta_\mu=1$, so $\mu=1$. $\sigma=\exp(\theta_\sigma x)$ with $\theta_\sigma=\ln2$, so $\sigma=2$. An action $a=5$ is sampled with advantage $\delta=+1$ and $\alpha=0.05$. Update both parameters.

  1. Mean score: $\dfrac{a-\mu}{\sigma^2}x=\dfrac{5-1}{4}\cdot1=1$.
  2. Std score: $\Big(\dfrac{(a-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2}-1\Big)x=\dfrac{16}{4}-1=3$.
  3. Updates ($\delta=1$): $\theta_\mu\leftarrow1+0.05\cdot1\cdot1=1.05$; $\theta_\sigma\leftarrow\ln2+0.05\cdot1\cdot3=0.693+0.15=0.843$.
  4. Result: $\mu=1.05$ (shifted toward 5) and $\sigma=e^{0.843}\approx2.32$ (wider: the good action was 2σ away, so explore more).
  5. If instead $a=1.5$ (close to the mean): std score $=\tfrac{0.25}{4}-1=-0.94<0$ ⇒ with $\delta>0$, $\sigma$ shrinks — a good action near the mean means exploitation.

Example C — one-step actor–critic

Problem

Tabular critic: $\hat v(S)=1.0$, $\hat v(S')=2.0$. $R=0.5$, $\gamma=0.9$, $\beta=0.2$, $\alpha=0.05$, $I=1$. Policy over two actions is $\pi=(0.5,0.5)$ with softmax preferences and the action taken was $a_2$.

  1. TD error: $\delta=0.5+0.9\cdot2.0-1.0=1.3$ (better than expected).
  2. Critic: $\hat v(S)\leftarrow1.0+0.2\cdot1.3\cdot1=1.26$.
  3. Actor score: $\nabla\ln\pi(a_2)=(0,1)-(0.5,0.5)=(-0.5,\,0.5)$.
  4. Actor: $\boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta+0.05\cdot1\cdot1.3\cdot(-0.5,0.5)=\boldsymbol\theta+(-0.0325,\,+0.0325)$ ⇒ $a_2$ becomes more likely.
  5. Next step: $I\leftarrow\gamma I=0.9$.
