Hyperparameters & step sizes

What each hyperparameter in Chapters 2–9 controls, what happens when it is too small or too large, and the book's guidance — with a special focus on constant vs varying step sizes.

1. The step size α — constant vs varying

Nearly every method uses the rule $\textit{New}\leftarrow\textit{Old}+\alpha[\textit{Target}-\textit{Old}]$ (2.4). The step size decides how much each new target moves the estimate, and therefore how the estimate weights old vs new data.

Robbins–Monro conditions — convergence with probability 1(2.7)
The core trade-off in one sentence

Decreasing step sizes (meeting 2.7) converge on stationary problems; constant step sizes keep tracking on nonstationary ones — and since RL targets are effectively nonstationary (the policy changes in GPI; bootstrapped targets change even for a fixed policy), the book mostly uses constant α. Decreasing sequences "often converge very slowly or need considerable tuning" and "are seldom used in applications".

Try it: step-size schedules on a noisy target

Targets are drawn from $\mathcal N(\mu_t, \sigma^2)$. With "jump", the true mean changes from 1 to 3 halfway (a nonstationary problem). Every estimator starts at $Q_1=0$.

3. Discount rate γ

  • Defines the problem: present value of a reward $k$ steps ahead is $\gamma^{k}$ (relative to the next reward). Effective horizon ≈ $1/(1-\gamma)$.
  • γ = 0 myopic; γ → 1 far-sighted. Can change the optimal policy (Ex 3.22: left vs right flips at γ = 0.5).
  • Needed (γ < 1) for continuing tasks to keep returns finite; episodic tasks may use γ = 1.
  • DP convergence rate ∝ γ (contraction). Linear TD error bound $\frac{1}{1-\gamma}$ grows as γ → 1.
  • Adding a constant $c$ to rewards adds $c/(1-\gamma)$ to all values (continuing) — but can change behaviour in episodic tasks.

5. DP parameters

  • θ: stop when the largest change in a sweep < θ. Policy iteration tolerates loose θ (greedy policy is often right early — Fig 4.1).
  • Evaluation sweeps per improvement: 1 = value iteration, ∞ = policy iteration, between = truncated PI.
  • Sweep order (in-place / asynchronous): strongly affects speed.

7. How to read a parameter study

  • Plot a summary of performance (e.g. average reward over the first 1000 steps, or RMS error over the first 10 episodes) against the parameter on a log scale (Figs 2.6, 6.3, 7.2, 9.2).
  • Expect an inverted U (reward) or U (error): too small learns or explores too little; too large is noisy or unstable.
  • Judge a method by its best value and by its sensitivity (width of the good region).
  • Distinguish interim (early learning) from asymptotic performance — e.g. Sarsa's asymptotic return collapses at large α in cliff walking while Expected Sarsa's does not.
