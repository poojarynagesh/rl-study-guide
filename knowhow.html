Multi-armed bandits

Asked in: Midterm 1 Q1 Tutorial 2 Q2 Jan 2021 Q2 Aug 2020 Q2 Jan 2021 Q3 Aug 2020 Q3 Tutorial 3 Q2 Tutorial 3 Q4

Know this cold
  • Judge an arm by its expected reward $\E[r]=\sum r\,P(r)$, never by its largest possible reward. A rare huge payout is the standard decoy.
  • ε-greedy with $k$ arms: greedy arm has probability $1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon/k$; every other arm has $\varepsilon/k$. The greedy arm also gets its share of the random pick.
  • Ties in $\argmax$: split the $(1-\varepsilon)$ mass equally among the $m$ tied arms, so each tied arm gets $(1-\varepsilon)/m+\varepsilon/k$.
  • UCB: $A_t=\argmax_a\big[Q_t(a)+c\sqrt{\ln t/N_t(a)}\big]$, natural log, $t$ = index of the step being decided. An arm with $N=0$ has infinite bonus and is tried first (lecture: UCB forces every arm to be played once).
  • Sample average (incremental): $Q_{n+1}=Q_n+\tfrac1n(R_n-Q_n)$. Needs only $Q$ and $N$ per arm. Good for stationary arms; the step $1/n\to0$ so it adapts slowly if the arm drifts.
  • Constant step $\alpha$: $Q\leftarrow Q+\alpha(R-Q)$. A reward received $j$ updates ago has weight $\alpha(1-\alpha)^j$; the initial value keeps weight $(1-\alpha)^n$. It tracks non-stationary arms but never fully converges (Robbins-Monro needs $\sum\alpha_n^2\lt\infty$).
  • Gradient bandit: $\pi_t(a)=e^{H_t(a)}/\sum_b e^{H_t(b)}$; $H_{t+1}(a)=H_t(a)+\alpha(R_t-\bar R_t)\big(\mathbb 1[a=A_t]-\pi_t(a)\big)$ for every arm $a$ (chosen and not chosen). Only differences of $H$ matter; $H\equiv0$ gives $\pi=1/k$. A baseline is free because $\sum_x\partial\pi(x)/\partial H(a)=0$.
  • Indicator trick: to find the arm with the largest $P(R\ge x)$, replace the reward by $Z=\mathbb 1\{R\ge x\}$; then $\E[Z]=P(R\ge x)$ and the ordinary bandit algorithm applies to $Z$.
Method — step by step
  1. "Which arm is best?" Compute $\E[r_i]$ for every arm; the answer is the arm with the largest mean. Add the variance if risk is asked.
  2. "Describe a procedure when distributions are unknown" (4 marks): initialise $Q=0,N=0$; choose by ε-greedy (or UCB); observe $R$; $N\leftarrow N+1$; $Q\leftarrow Q+\frac1N(R-Q)$; argue $Q\to\E[r]$ by the law of large numbers and that $\varepsilon\gt0$ samples every arm infinitely often. Write all four lines; the marks are for the update rule and the justification.
  3. UCB question: compute $\ln t$ once, then $Q(a)+c\sqrt{\ln t/N(a)}$ for every arm, pick the max. State the chosen arm and why (largest optimistic estimate), not just the numbers.
  4. ε-greedy probabilities: find the greedy arm(s) first, then fill the table and check that it sums to 1.
  5. Calculus on $\E[R]$: write $f(p)=\E[R]$ as a function of the parameter, solve $f'(p)=0$, confirm $f''\lt0$, check the endpoints of the interval.
  6. Non-stationary: switch to constant $\alpha$, state the weight $\alpha(1-\alpha)^j$ and why $1/N$ fails.

If the question says…Do this
"You are told only the possible rewards, not the probabilities — which arm most/least?" (Tutorial 2 Q2a)Gamble on the arm with the biggest possible reward (naive guess) and say explicitly that it ignores probabilities; part (c), after the means, reverses it.

"Give action probabilities under ε-greedy"Greedy $1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon/k$, others $\varepsilon/k$; if two arms share the max, split $(1-\varepsilon)$ between them.
"After this update, recalculate the ε-greedy probabilities" (Tutorial 3 Q4c)Update $Q$ first, then check whether the $\argmax$ changed. If the greedy arm is unchanged the probabilities are unchanged.
"Which arm does UCB select?" with counts $N$ givenEvaluate $U(a)=Q(a)+c\sqrt{\ln t/N(a)}$ for every arm. The least-tried arm often wins even with a lower $Q$.
"Rewards are non-stationary" / "Compare constant $\alpha=0.25$ with the sample average" (Midterm 1 Q1c, Tutorial 3 Q2)Run $V\leftarrow V+\alpha(G-V)$ step by step; the sample average of $n$ values ignores the start value; constant $\alpha$ keeps $(1-\alpha)^n$ of it.
"The best arm is the one with the largest $P(R\ge10)$" (Aug 2020 Q3)Indicator $Z=\mathbb 1\{R\ge10\}$, then $Q(a)\leftarrow Q(a)+\frac1{N(a)}(Z-Q(a))$ with ε-greedy or UCB selection; the answer is the arm with the largest $Q$. For $R\in[a,b]$ use that indicator.
"Reward $=1-p$ with probability $p$, else 0; which $p$ is best?" (Aug 2020 Q2, Jan 2021 Q3)$\E[R]=p(1-p)$; table for the given arms; calculus gives $p=\tfrac12$, maximum $\tfrac14$, $f''=-2$.
"Write/derive the gradient-bandit update"State softmax, the update for chosen and non-chosen arms, and the role of the baseline $\bar R$ (variance reduction, no bias).
Traps and tricky points
  • Greedy share trap: $P(\text{greedy})=1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon/k$, not $1-\varepsilon$. With $\varepsilon=0.1,k=3$ it is $0.9333$, not $0.9$. The probability that the chosen arm is non-greedy is only $\varepsilon(k-1)/k$.
  • Large payoff, tiny probability: arm 3 in Tutorial 2 has the biggest reward (200) but the smallest mean (4.32).
  • UCB with $N=0$: division by zero means "pick it first"; do not skip such an arm.
  • UCB is not best-$Q$: in Midterm 1 the arm with $Q=1.2$ beats the arm with $Q=1.4$ because it was pulled once.
  • Larger $c$ means more exploration; the bonus shrinks as $N$ grows.
  • Sample average with $\alpha=1/n$: after $n$ returns the answer is the plain mean regardless of the start value; if your $1/n$ answer differs from the plain mean you made an error.
  • Constant $\alpha$ puts weight $(1-\alpha)^n$ on the initial estimate, so a bad start biases it (optimistic initial values exploit this to force early exploration).
  • Gradient bandit: non-chosen arms move the opposite way by $\alpha(R-\bar R)\pi(b)$. Forgetting that part is the common error.
  • A linear expected reward (gain $a$ with probability $p$, loss $-b$ otherwise) has its optimum at an endpoint, not at an interior stationary point.

Returns and discounting

Asked in: Midterm 1 Q3 Aug 2020 Q5 Tutorial 3 Q1 Final 2025 Q2e

Know this cold
  • $G_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma R_{t+2}+\gamma^2R_{t+3}+\cdots$ and the recursion $G_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma G_{t+1}$ with $G_T=0$ at termination. This identity is the root of every Bellman equation.
  • The return after a state starts with the reward on the arrow leaving it.
  • Constant reward $r$ forever: $G=r/(1-\gamma)$. The same reward starting only after $k$ steps: $\gamma^kr/(1-\gamma)$.
  • Periodic rewards $(r_1,\dots,r_n)$ repeating: $G_0=\dfrac{r_1+\gamma r_2+\cdots+\gamma^{n-1}r_n}{1-\gamma^n}$ (block sum times a geometric series in $\gamma^n$). Pattern $(2,-1)$: $\frac{2-\gamma}{1-\gamma^2}$.
  • Bounded rewards $|R|\le B$ and $\gamma\lt1$ give $|G_t|\le B/(1-\gamma)$. At $\gamma=1$ in a continuing task the sum may be infinite, and infinite returns cannot be ranked (lecture: reward 1 forever and rewards $1,\tfrac12,\tfrac13,\dots$ both give $\infty$).
  • $\gamma=0$: only the immediate reward counts (myopic). Larger $\gamma$ makes the agent far-sighted. Episodic tasks may use $\gamma=1$ if termination is reached in finite time.
  • The optimal policy depends on $\gamma$: write the value of each policy as a function of $\gamma$ and compare; the best policy can flip at a crossover $\gamma$.
Method — step by step
  1. Finite episode: start at the end, $G_T=0$, and work backwards with $G_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma G_{t+1}$. Tabulate $G_t$ for every $t$; first-visit and every-visit MC then just pick entries.
  2. Periodic reward stream: group one period, sum with powers $1,\gamma,\dots,\gamma^{n-1}$, divide by $1-\gamma^n$. For $G_1$ shift the pattern (start from the second reward) and verify $G_0=R_1+\gamma G_1$.
  3. Effect of $\gamma$ on the optimal policy: for each deterministic policy write the Bellman equations of its cycle, solve for the value at the decision state, compare numerically at the $\gamma$ values asked, then solve $v_A(\gamma)=v_B(\gamma)$ for the crossover.
  4. $\gamma\to1$: the numerator tends to the block sum and the denominator to $0^+$. A non-zero block sum gives $\pm\infty$; a zero block sum gives a finite limit (take it with L'Hôpital).

If the question says…Do this
"Rewards are $3,-2,1,3,-2,1,\dots$; derive a closed form for $G_0$" (Midterm 1 Q3)$G_0=\dfrac{3-2\gamma+\gamma^2}{1-\gamma^3}$; show the block-and-geometric-series step.
"Compute $G_1$ and verify $G_0=R_1+\gamma G_1$"$G_1=\dfrac{-2+\gamma+3\gamma^2}{1-\gamma^3}$; plug numbers into both sides.
"What happens as $\gamma\uparrow1$?"The block sum is $2\ne0$, so $G_0\to+\infty$; the undiscounted infinite return does not exist, hence $\gamma\lt1$ or episodic.
"Compute the return after each occurrence of $A$" (Tutorial 3 Q1)Backward recursion; first-visit uses the first occurrence only, every-visit averages all.
"Which policy is optimal if $\gamma=0$? $0.5$? $0.9$?" (Aug 2020 Q5)Write both cycle values, compare, and report ties as "both optimal" (see the worked example).
"Find the $\gamma$ at which the optimal policy changes"Equate the two value expressions. If they share the factor $1/(1-\gamma^2)\gt0$, compare the numerators only. Different cycle lengths: solve the polynomial equation (standard result).
"A reward $+r$ arrives only at step $k$, or from step $k$ on"Single reward $R_k$: weight $\gamma^{k-1}$ in $G_0$. Perpetual from step $k$: $\gamma^{k-1}r/(1-\gamma)$. Check the indexing before writing the power.
Traps and tricky points
  • Indexing: $R_{t+1}$ is the first reward of $G_t$ and is not discounted; $R_{t+2}$ gets $\gamma$.
  • Use the same denominator when comparing two periodic policies; mixing $1-\gamma$ with $1-\gamma^2$ without simplifying gives wrong crossovers.
  • A tie (both policies equal at the crossover) means both are optimal, not "neither".
  • The optimal policy is decided by the value at the decision state; the bottom states of the cycles have different values (e.g. $v_{\text{left}}(L)=4+\gamma v_{\text{left}}(T)$) but are not compared.
  • $G_t$ is not monotone in $t$; do not assume it decreases because $\gamma\lt1$ (the Exercise 3.8 sequence goes $2,6,8,4,2,0$).
  • With $\gamma=1$ the lecture rule is: allowed only for episodic tasks that terminate with probability one under the policy; do not apply $r/(1-\gamma)$ there.

Bellman equations

Asked in: Final 2025 Q1 Tutorial 2 Q3 Jan 2021 Q5 Tutorial 2 Q4 Aug 2020 Q4 Final 2025 Q2a Jan 2021 Q4a,c

Know this cold
  • (a) $q_\pi(s,a)=\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\,[r+\gamma v_\pi(s')]$.
  • (b) $v_\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\,q_\pi(s,a)$. Direction: $\pi$ averages over actions (agent), $p$ averages over $(s',r)$ (environment).
  • (c) $v_\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)[r+\gamma v_\pi(s')]$.
  • (e) Q-Bellman: $q_\pi(s,a)=\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma\sum_{a'}\pi(a'\mid s')q_\pi(s',a')\big]$. Yes, it exists.
  • Optimality forms: $v_*(s)=\max_a\sum p[r+\gamma v_*(s')]$; $q_*(s,a)=\sum p[r+\gamma\max_{a'}q_*(s',a')]$; $v_*(s)=\max_aq_*(s,a)$.
  • 3-argument form with $r(s,a)$ and $p(s'\mid s,a)$: $q(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)v(s')$. Deterministic MDP: $q(s,a)=r+\gamma v(s')$.
  • Existence/uniqueness: $T_\pi v=r_\pi+\gamma P_\pi v$ is a $\gamma$-contraction in $\|\cdot\|_\infty$; Banach gives a unique fixed point, and since $v_\pi$ satisfies the equation (law of total expectation applied to $G_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma G_{t+1}$), the solution is $v_\pi$. The iteration $v_{k+1}=T_\pi v_k$ converges from any start.
  • Matrix form: $v=r_\pi+\gamma P_\pi v\Rightarrow v=(I-\gamma P_\pi)^{-1}r_\pi$; invertible because the spectral radius of $\gamma P_\pi$ is $\le\gamma\lt1$ (Neumann series $\sum_k(\gamma P_\pi)^k$).
  • $v(\text{terminal})=0$ always. The optimal value $v_*$ is unique; optimal policies need not be (ties); there are $|\mathcal A|^{|\mathcal S|}$ deterministic policies.
Method — step by step
  1. Decide the type: a given policy means the expectation form (weights $\pi$); "optimal" means $\max$ over actions.
  2. Build a table of $(s,a,s',r,p)$ rows first. The reward is attached to the transition, so the expected one-step reward of an action is $\sum p\,r$.
  3. Write one equation per non-terminal state; terminal states contribute 0.
  4. Contraction proof (2 marks): $|T_\pi u(s)-T_\pi v(s)|=\gamma\big|\sum_a\pi\sum p\,(u(s')-v(s'))\big|\le\gamma\|u-v\|_\infty$ because the rewards cancel and the weights $\pi p$ sum to 1. For the optimality operator also use $|\max_af(a)-\max_ag(a)|\le\max_a|f(a)-g(a)|$.
  5. Conclude: complete space of bounded functions, Banach fixed-point theorem, unique solution, identify it as $v_\pi$ (or $v_*$).
  6. Solve by substitution or $(I-\gamma P_\pi)^{-1}r_\pi$, then plug the answer back into every equation as a check.

If the question says…Do this
"Give $q_\pi$ in terms of $v_\pi$ and the four-argument $p$"Formula (a). Check that the bracket contains $r+\gamma v_\pi(s')$, summed over $(s',r)$.

"Write the Bellman equation for the equiprobable policy and solve" (Tutorial 2 Q4c, Jan 2021 Q4a)Average the actions with weights $\pi$; solve the linear system; verify.
"Write the Bellman equation for the optimal policy" (Final Q2a, Jan 2021 Q4c)Replace the policy average by $\max$; a state with a single action has no $\max$.
"Write $q$ using the 3-argument $p$ and $r(s,a)$"$q=r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)v(s')$. Expected reward $r(s,a)=\sum_{s',r}r\,p(s',r\mid s,a)$.
"Write in matrix form"$v=r_\pi+\gamma P_\pi v$, solution $(I-\gamma P_\pi)^{-1}r_\pi$; define $P_\pi(s,s')=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)p(s'\mid s,a)$.
"Can the policy be recovered from $q_*$ alone?"Yes, greedy in $q_*$, no model needed. From $v_*$ alone you need the model for the one-step lookahead (standard result).
Traps and tricky points
  • Expectation vs optimality: using $\max$ when a policy is given (or the reverse) is the most common error. Name which type you write.
  • Reward attached to the transition: in the four-room MDP $A_1$ in state 1 has expected reward $0.3\cdot1=0.3$, not 1; action $A$ in state 2 has $0.3\cdot0.5=0.15$.
  • $\sum_{s',r}$ in the 4-argument form sums over pairs; in the 3-argument form the reward is outside the sum.
  • Terminal states: $v=0$, and an absorbing goal with $\gamma=1$ is what keeps the sums finite.
  • Do not forget the $\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)$ in (c); a bare $\sum_{s',r}$ describes only a fixed action.
  • Uniqueness holds for $v_*$ but not for $\pi_*$ (ties allowed); the lecture example is state $A$ where all actions lead to $A'$ with +10, so all tie.
  • "Contraction" needs a norm: the sup-norm. The $\max$ operator is non-expansive, which is why the optimality operator is still a $\gamma$-contraction.

Policy evaluation

Asked in: Aug 2020 Q6 Midterm 1 Q2 Tutorial 2 Q4 Final 2025 Q2d

Know this cold
  • Policy evaluation computes $v_\pi$ for a fixed $\pi$. Two ways: exact (solve the $n$ linear equations $v=r_\pi+\gamma P_\pi v$) or iterative $v_{k+1}(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)[r+\gamma v_k(s')]$ from $v_0=0$ (or any start).
  • Synchronous (two-array): every state in sweep $k+1$ uses only $v_k$. In-place (Gauss-Seidel): uses already-updated values within the sweep, so the numbers depend on the state order given in the question. Both converge to $v_\pi$.
  • Stopping: $\Delta\leftarrow\max_s|v_{\text{new}}(s)-v_{\text{old}}(s)|$ over a full sweep; stop when $\Delta\lt\theta$. The initial guess does not change the limit, only the speed.
  • For $\gamma\lt1$ the iteration error shrinks like $\gamma^k\|v_0-v_\pi\|_\infty$ (standard result, from the contraction).
  • Greedy action at sweep $k$: $\argmax_a\sum p[r+\gamma v_k(s')]$, computed from $v_k$ including the reward. At $v_0=0$ every action ties unless the rewards differ.
  • Symmetry: states that are mirror images under a symmetry of the MDP and policy have equal values at every sweep; use it to cut work and as a check.
Method — step by step
  1. List the actions with their $\pi$ weights and, for each state, the (reward, next state) of every action. Mark goals/terminals with $v=0$.
  2. Exact: one linear equation per state, eliminate by substitution (use symmetry to reduce the unknowns), then verify in every equation.
  3. Iterative: write the sweep formula once, then compute $v_1,v_2,\dots$ as a table with one column per state; keep the previous row visible.
  4. For each cell write the four terms $r+\gamma v_k(s')$ before averaging; off-grid moves keep $s'=s$ with their (penalty) reward.
  5. Greedy action at each $k$: for each state compare $r(s,a)+\gamma v_k(s')$ over actions; list all tied actions.
  6. State the stopping rule ($\Delta\lt\theta$) and, if asked, compute $\Delta$ for the last sweep.

Traps and tricky points
  • Wall penalty: an off-grid action gives reward $-2$ and the state does not change; the next value is $v_k(s)$ itself, not $v_k$ of a neighbour. State 4 (interior) never hits a wall.
  • A move into a goal still gets the step reward $-1$ and next value $0$.
  • Greedy at $k=0$: all zeros means all actions tie; the greedy policy is meaningless until $v_k$ carries information.
  • Greedy w.r.t. $v_\pi$ is one improvement step, not necessarily the optimal policy.
  • Do not mix sync and in-place; read the question for the sweep order. $v_k$ in the table means after $k$ full sweeps.

Policy improvement and policy iteration

Asked in: Midterm 1 Q2b Midterm 1 Q5 Final 2025 Q2 (variation) Aug 2020 Q6b

Know this cold
  • Improvement step: $\pi'(s)=\argmax_aq_\pi(s,a)$ with $q_\pi(s,a)=\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)[r+\gamma v_\pi(s')]$. Deterministic MDP: $q_\pi(s,a)=r+\gamma v_\pi(s')$.
  • Policy improvement theorem: if $q_\pi(s,\pi'(s))\ge v_\pi(s)$ for all $s$ then $v_{\pi'}\ge v_\pi$ everywhere (strictly somewhere if the inequality is strict somewhere).
  • Policy iteration: evaluate $\pi$ exactly, improve greedily, repeat. Terminate when the policy is stable ($\pi'=\pi$).
  • Why stable means optimal (lecture 12, §8): if the greedy $\pi'$ has $v_{\pi'}=v_\pi$ then $v_\pi(s)=\max_a\sum p[r+\gamma v_\pi(s')]$, which is the Bellman optimality equation, whose unique solution is $v_*$. It is not a mere local optimum.
  • Number of deterministic policies $=|\mathcal A|^{|\mathcal S|}$ (3 states, 2 actions: 8). Each improvement is at least as good, so with consistent tie-breaking PI terminates in finitely many outer steps. The inner evaluation may need many sweeps.
  • Ties: keep the current action unless another is strictly better. Alternatively two policies can differ yet have the same value everywhere, which is also a stopping sign (lecture).
  • Stochastic $\pi$: $v_\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)q_\pi(s,a)$; so $q_\pi(s,\pi(s))=v_\pi(s)$ for a deterministic $\pi$, a free sanity check.
Method — step by step
  1. Evaluate $\pi$ exactly (linear system, substitute and verify).
  2. For every state and every action compute $q_\pi(s,a)$; fill a table.
  3. Check that the current action's $q$ equals $v_\pi(s)$ (consistency).
  4. $\pi'(s)=\argmax_aq_\pi(s,a)$; list which states changed. Apply the tie rule.
  5. "Is it optimal?" Not yet: evaluate $\pi'$ and improve again. If nothing changes, state "policy stable, so $v_\pi$ satisfies Bellman optimality, so $\pi=\pi_*$."
  6. If only a partial question is asked ("improvement at state D"), still compute $q$ for all actions in that state and say whether to switch.

Traps and tricky points
  • "Policy changed" does not mean "optimal". You must show that the next improvement gives no change.
  • An improvement step must compare against all actions, including ones the current policy never uses.
  • Compare $q_\pi(s,a)$ values, not rewards. In Midterm 1 Q2 the immediate reward of Operate (2) beats Maintain ($-2$), but $q_\pi(D,\text{Maintain})=33.39\gt q_\pi(D,\text{Operate})=20.03$.
  • Ties: switching between equally good actions can cycle forever; use a fixed tie-break or "keep current".
  • Improvement is evaluated against the old $v_\pi$; do not update $v$ inside the improvement step.
  • $v_{\pi'}\ge v_\pi$ is guaranteed; $v_{\pi'}\gt v_\pi$ in every state is not.
  • The optimal value is unique, the optimal policy may not be.

Value iteration

Asked in: Final 2025 Q2b,c Midterm 1 Q4 Tutorial 2 Q4d Jan 2021 Q4c,d

Know this cold
  • Update: $V_{k+1}(s)=\max_a\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)[r+\gamma V_k(s')]$; deterministic: $\max_a[r(s,a)+\gamma V_k(s')]$. It is the Bellman optimality equation as an assignment. Start $V_0=0$, $V(\text{terminal})=0$.
  • Synchronous: every state uses the old vector $V_k$ (two arrays). In-place (Gauss-Seidel) gives different intermediate numbers.
  • Stopping rule: sweep all states, $\Delta=\max_s|V_{k+1}(s)-V_k(s)|$; stop when $\Delta\lt\theta$. Output the greedy policy $\pi(s)=\argmax_a\sum p[r+\gamma V(s')]$.
  • What $\Delta\lt\theta$ guarantees (standard result; the bound is quoted in the Midterm 1 solutions): $\|V_{k+1}-V_*\|_\infty\le\dfrac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\|V_{k+1}-V_k\|_\infty$. So $\Delta\lt\theta$ gives error $\le\gamma\theta/(1-\gamma)$; to guarantee error $\varepsilon$ choose $\theta=\varepsilon(1-\gamma)/\gamma$. Also $\|V_k-V_*\|\le\gamma^k\|V_0-V_*\|$.
  • The policy stabilises before the values converge. The greedy policy w.r.t. $V_k$ is generally not guaranteed optimal for finite $k$. It is optimal once it is greedy w.r.t. $V_*$.
  • Verify optimality of a candidate: (i) the candidate $v$ satisfies the Bellman optimality equation in every state; or (ii) evaluate the candidate policy and policy improvement gives no change.
  • $\gamma=1$: convergence needs termination (absorbing goals, proper policies, $V(\text{terminal})=0$); the contraction argument no longer applies.
  • VI vs PI: a VI sweep costs $O(|\mathcal S|^2|\mathcal A|)$, there is no explicit policy until the end, and many iterations are needed; PI needs an evaluation (many sweeps or a linear solve) per iteration but few iterations. Modified PI interposes a few sweeps.
Method — step by step
  1. Write the update formula with $V_k$ on the right and $V_{k+1}$ on the left, and state "synchronous, $V_0=0$".
  2. For each state compute the action values $[r+\gamma V_k(s')]$ for every action in a small table; $V_{k+1}$ is the row maximum.
  3. Record both the new $V$ and the maximising action(s); the policy "greedy w.r.t. $V_k$" is read from the table built from $V_k$.
  4. Compute $\Delta_k=\max_s|V_{k+1}-V_k|$; compare with $\theta$ if asked.
  5. "Is the greedy policy optimal?" Say: no guarantee unless it is greedy w.r.t. $V_*$; show $V_*$ (solve the optimality equations or iterate further) and compare.
  6. For threshold questions ("smallest $L$..."), write the two competing action values as functions of $L$ and solve the inequality.

Monte Carlo prediction and control

Asked in: Tutorial 3 Q1 Tutorial 3 Q2 Tutorial 3 Q4 Final 2025 Q2(e) Lecture 13 (ten-step example) Lecture 14 (exploring starts)

Know this cold
  • Return: $G_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma R_{t+2}+\dots=R_{t+1}+\gamma G_{t+1}$, with $G_T=0$. The return after a visit starts at the reward you get on leaving the state, $R_{t+1}$, never at the reward received on entering it.
  • MC prediction = average the returns observed after visits to $s$: $\hat V(s)=\frac1N\sum_i G^{(i)}(s)$. Model-free, needs complete episodes, does not bootstrap (the target contains no estimate).
  • First-visit: only the first occurrence of $s$ in each episode gives a sample (the count resets every episode). Every-visit: every occurrence gives a sample. Both converge to $v_\pi$.
  • First-visit samples (one per episode) are independent across episodes; every-visit returns inside one episode overlap and are correlated, and every-visit is biased for finite data (standard result). Every-visit extracts more data per episode, so it suits scarce episodes.
  • Incremental sample average: $V\leftarrow V+\frac1{N}(G-V)$, where $N$ counts the eligible visits (first-visits or all visits). Constant step: $V\leftarrow V+\alpha(G-V)$ gives an exponentially weighted average: weight on the $k$-th of $n$ returns is $\alpha(1-\alpha)^{n-k}$, weight left on the initial value is $(1-\alpha)^n$. With $\alpha=1/n$ the initial value is forgotten after the first update.
  • Robbins-Monro: $\sum\alpha_n=\infty$ and $\sum\alpha_n^2\lt\infty$ for convergence. Constant $\alpha$ violates the second, so it keeps tracking (good for non-stationary problems) but never settles (standard result).
  • Control needs $Q$, not $V$: without the model $p$ you cannot do the one-step look-ahead from $V$. Improvement is then model-free: $\pi'(s)=\arg\max_a Q(s,a)$.
  • Exploration problem: a deterministic $\pi$ never generates $(s,a_2)$ for $a_2\ne\pi(s)$. Fixes: exploring starts (every pair has positive probability of being the first pair, then follow $\pi$) or $\varepsilon$-soft policies.
  • $\varepsilon$-soft: $\pi(a\mid s)\ge\varepsilon/|\mathcal A|$. $\varepsilon$-greedy: greedy action $1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon/|\mathcal A|$, every other action $\varepsilon/|\mathcal A|$.
Method — step by step
  1. Write the episode as a table: $t$, $S_t$, $R_{t+1}$ (reward on the arrow leaving $S_t$).
  2. Compute returns backwards: $G_T=0$, $G_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma G_{t+1}$. Fill a column $G_t$ for every $t$.
  3. Pick the samples. First-visit: for each state keep only the smallest $t$ with $S_t=s$. Every-visit: keep all $t$ with $S_t=s$.
  4. Average. Several episodes: first-visit divides by the number of episodes containing $s$, every-visit divides by the total number of visits.
  5. If the question gives a step size, do not average; apply $V\leftarrow V+\alpha(G-V)$ in the order the returns arrive and write each intermediate value.
  6. Control (exploring starts): initialise $Q$ and a deterministic $\pi$; start an episode at a random $(S_0,A_0)$, follow $\pi$ afterwards; sweep $t=T-1,\dots,0$: $G\leftarrow\gamma G+R_{t+1}$; if $(S_t,A_t)$ is a first visit, update $Q(S_t,A_t)$ (average) and set $\pi(S_t)\leftarrow\arg\max_a Q(S_t,a)$.
  7. Control ($\varepsilon$-soft, no exploring starts): same loop, but the improvement step makes $\pi(\cdot\mid S_t)$ $\varepsilon$-greedy w.r.t. $Q$ instead of purely greedy.

  • In MC control, first-visit applies to the pair $(s,a)$, not just to $s$.
  • Do not mix "average of returns" (step $1/N$) with "constant $\alpha$" in one answer; with $\alpha=1/n$ they coincide exactly (check: after four returns the result is exactly the sample mean 6).
  • Exploring-starts guarantees are for the true $Q_\pi$; with a few noisy returns the greedy policy can flip prematurely.

Off-policy Monte Carlo and importance sampling

Asked in: Tutorial 3 Q3 Tutorial 3 Q5 Lecture 14 (coverage) Lecture 15 (ordinary vs weighted) Lecture 21 (off-policy n-step ratio)

Know this cold
  • Data come from behaviour policy $b$, you want $v_\pi$ for target policy $\pi$. Averaging raw returns gives $v_b$, not $v_\pi$.
  • Coverage: $\pi(a\mid s)\gt0\Rightarrow b(a\mid s)\gt0$. $b$ may have extra actions; $\pi$ may not. If it fails, no amount of data helps.
  • Ratio: $\rho_{t:T-1}=\prod_{k=t}^{T-1}\dfrac{\pi(A_k\mid S_k)}{b(A_k\mid S_k)}$. The environment factors $p(S_{k+1},R_{k+1}\mid S_k,A_k)$ appear in numerator and denominator and cancel, so no model is needed, only the two action-probability tables.
  • Identity: $\mathbb E_b[\rho_{t:T-1}G_t\mid S_t=s]=v_\pi(s)$.
  • $\rho\gt1$: the observed actions are more likely under $\pi$ (up-weight); $0\lt\rho\lt1$: down-weight; $\rho=0$: some action is impossible under $\pi$, the episode contributes nothing; $b=\pi$ gives $\rho=1$ and MC is on-policy.
  • Ordinary IS: $\hat V=\frac1n\sum_i\rho_iG_i$. Unbiased (first-visit), but variance can be huge or infinite.
  • Weighted IS: $\hat V=\dfrac{\sum_i\rho_iG_i}{\sum_i\rho_i}$. Biased at finite $n$ (bias vanishes as $n\to\infty$, so consistent), much lower variance, always a convex combination of the observed returns. For the first sample the estimate equals $G$ whatever $\rho$ is. Undefined ($0/0$) if every $\rho$ is zero.
  • Incremental weighted IS: $C_n=C_{n-1}+W_n$, $\;V_{n+1}=V_n+\dfrac{W_n}{C_n}(G_n-V_n)$ (a data-dependent step size $W_n/C_n$).
  • Per-decision IS: weight each reward only by the ratios up to its own time, $\sum_k\gamma^k\rho_{0:k}R_{k+1}$; same expectation, lower variance.
  • Off-policy MC control (standard result, Sutton and Barto §5.7): $\pi$ greedy w.r.t. $Q$, $b$ any soft policy; sweep backwards with $W\leftarrow1$, update $Q$ by weighted IS, stop the sweep when $A_t\ne\pi(S_t)$, otherwise $W\leftarrow W/b(A_t\mid S_t)$ (because $\pi(A_t\mid S_t)=1$).
Method — step by step
  1. Make a table for each episode: $t$, $\pi(A_t\mid S_t)$, $b(A_t\mid S_t)$, ratio $\pi/b$.
  2. Multiply the ratios over the steps that matter: from the time of the visit to the end of the episode (for a first-visit at $t=0$ that is the whole episode).
  3. Compute the (discounted) return $G$ from the same time index, backwards.
  4. Weighted return per episode: $\rho G$. Ordinary estimate: sum of $\rho G$ divided by the number of episodes. Weighted estimate: sum of $\rho G$ divided by the sum of $\rho$.
  5. Sanity check: weighted IS must lie between the smallest and largest observed return; ordinary IS may fall outside.
  6. If asked to do it incrementally, keep $C$ and $V$ in a two-column table, starting from $C_0=0$.
  7. Compare and comment on bias and variance in one sentence each.

Traps and tricky points
  • Ratio is $\pi/b$, target on top. Inverting it is the commonest slip.
  • The ratio starts at the time of the visit, not at time 0. For a later (every-visit) occurrence use $\rho_{t:T-1}$ with that $t$.
  • For action values $Q(s,a)$ the first action is given (the pair is conditioned on), so the ratio starts at $t+1$ (lecture 21, off-policy n-step Sarsa; standard for off-policy MC prediction of $Q$).
  • The return $G$ is the one observed under $b$; you never recompute rewards under $\pi$.
  • Ordinary IS: divide by the number of samples (zero-ratio episodes count). Weighted IS: divide by $\sum\rho$.
  • Weighted IS with a single sample returns $G$ itself; do not expect it to "use" $\rho$ then.
  • Variance source (lecture 15): one factor like $0.8/0.01=80$ in a long trajectory blows the product up; a few episodes then dominate the average.
  • Per-decision and full-trajectory weighted returns agree in expectation but not sample by sample. In Tutorial 3 Q5 both happen to be $8.25$, which is a coincidence, not a rule.
  • Unbiased does not mean good: ordinary IS is unbiased yet can be far worse in practice than the biased weighted IS.
  • Off-policy is slower and noisier than on-policy; its benefit is reusing logs and learning a deterministic optimal target while exploring (lecture 14 traffic-signal example).

TD(0) prediction

Asked in: Tutorial 3 Q6 Tutorial 3 Q8 Tutorial 3 Q9 Final 2025 Q2(e) variation (TD(0) on the episode) Final 2025 Q4 Lecture 16 (MC error = sum of TD errors) Lecture 17 (batch TD vs batch MC)

Know this cold
  • TD error: $\delta_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma V(S_{t+1})-V(S_t)$. Update: $V(S_t)\leftarrow V(S_t)+\alpha\,\delta_t$. Only the state just left is updated.
  • TD target $R_{t+1}+\gamma V(S_{t+1})$ bootstraps (contains an estimate). MC target $G_t$ does not. Both fit "new = old + $\alpha$(target - old)".
  • $V(\text{terminal})=0$, so the last transition's target is just $R$.
  • TD updates after every transition (works for continuing tasks, no waiting); MC waits for the end of the episode.
  • Bias and variance: MC target unbiased but high variance; TD target biased (depends on current estimates) but low variance (standard result, noted in lecture 16 as "standard context").
  • MC error as TD errors (V held fixed): $G_t-V(S_t)=\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}\gamma^{k-t}\delta_k$. Proof: $G_t-V(S_t)=\delta_t+\gamma[G_{t+1}-V(S_{t+1})]$, then recurse.
  • Batch updating: sweep the same finite data repeatedly. Batch MC converges to the sample average of observed returns (best fit to returns). Batch TD converges to the value of the maximum-likelihood Markov model built from the empirical transitions. They differ when states share successors.
  • Semi-gradient TD(0) with linear features (Final 2025 Q4): $w\leftarrow w+\alpha\,\delta\,x(s)$, $\delta=r+\gamma w^\top x(s')-w^\top x(s)$, with $\hat v(\text{terminal})=0$. Tabular TD is the special case $X=I$.
Method — step by step
  1. Write the transitions $(S,R,S')$ in time order as a table with columns: $V(S)$ before, target, $\delta$, $\alpha\delta$, $V(S)$ after.
  2. Decide the mode from the wording. "Using the initial estimates / independently": freeze $V$, compute every $\delta$ from the original values (Tutorial 3 Q6, Q9). "Run through the episode / update in order / using the latest value": online, each update overwrites $V$ before the next transition (Tutorial 3 Q8).
  3. For each transition: $\delta=R+\gamma V(S')-V(S)$, with $V(S')=0$ if $S'$ is terminal.
  4. Update $V(S)\leftarrow V(S)+\alpha\delta$. Cross out the old value and write the new one.
  5. If a state repeats in the episode, it is updated twice, and the second update sees the first one.
  6. To compare with MC: compute $G$ backwards for the same start state and quote both targets (TD target vs MC target), then say which one trusts an estimate.
  7. With features: compute $\delta$ with dot products, update the whole vector $w$ by $\alpha\delta x(s)$.

Traps and tricky points
  • "Using the initial estimates" means all $\delta$ are computed from the original $V$, i.e. a frozen pass. It differs from the online pass only when a state repeats or a later transition starts at a state updated earlier. Tutorial 3 Q6 ($A,B,C$ distinct): online equals frozen.
  • Online with a repeated state (Tutorial 3 Q8): $V(A)=1.25,V(B)=4.75$. Frozen (all $\delta$ from $V(A)=2,V(B)=4$, summing the two $A$ increments): $V(A)=2.0$, $V(B)=4.0$. Know which one the wording asks for.
  • The first update of the episode uses the old $V(S')$; the update of $S'$ comes one step later.
  • $V(T)=0$. Forgetting it, or using the "initial" 0.5 for a terminal, is a classic error. Lecture 16: $A\to F$ with all values 0.5 gives $0.5+0.1(0+0-0.5)=0.45$.
  • A TD update changes only the departed state. In the random walk $C\to B\to A\to F$ (all 0.5, $\alpha=0.1$) only $V(A)$ moves, to 0.45; $C$ and $B$ stay at 0.5 because current and next estimates are equal.
  • MC vs TD one-pass values disagree (TD $1.25$ vs MC $2$ in Q8) simply because TD has not converged; both go to $v_\pi$ with more data.
  • Linear FA: if the question does not say $s_4$ is terminal, use $w^\top x(s_4)$. In Final Q4, if $s_4$ were an ordinary state the last $\delta$ would be $0+0.8\cdot2-4=-2.4$ and $w=(0.8,2.6,0.8)$ instead of $(0,2.6,0)$.
  • Rank-deficient features: predictions can still be defined but $w$ is not unique.
  • Do not claim TD is "unbiased": its target depends on the current estimate. Do not claim MC is "low variance".
  • Sum-of-TD-errors identity: it is exact only for a fixed $V$; with online updates the time index of $V$ matters.

SARSA, Expected SARSA, Q-learning and Double Q-learning

Asked in: Final 2025 Q3 Tutorial 3 Q7 Tutorial 3 Q9 Final 2025 Q3 variations (ε-greedy π, Double Q, n-step) Lecture 18 (cliff, Expected SARSA) Lecture 19 (maximization bias)

Know this cold
  • All three have the same shape: $Q(S,A)\leftarrow Q(S,A)+\alpha[\text{target}-Q(S,A)]$. Only the next-state term differs.
  • SARSA: target $R+\gamma Q(S',A')$, with $A'$ the action actually taken next (from the episode, chosen by the same $\varepsilon$-greedy policy). Needs the 5-tuple $S,A,R,S',A'$. On-policy.
  • Expected SARSA: target $R+\gamma\sum_b\pi(b\mid S')Q(S',b)$. Removes the sampling noise of $A'$. On-policy if $\pi$ is the behaviour policy, off-policy if $\pi$ differs; with $\pi$ greedy it is Q-learning.
  • Q-learning: target $R+\gamma\max_b Q(S',b)$. Off-policy: behaviour $\varepsilon$-greedy, target greedy; the maximising action need not be the next executed one. Correct form has $\gamma$ in front of the max (the board slip in lecture 18).
  • Terminal $S'$: every $Q(S',\cdot)=0$, so all three targets reduce to $R$.
  • $\varepsilon$-greedy expectation: greedy action weight $1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon/|\mathcal A|$, others $\varepsilon/|\mathcal A|$.
  • When SARSA = Q-learning: the sampled $A'$ is the argmax (Tutorial 3 Q7d), or the policy is greedy. When Expected SARSA = Q-learning: $\pi$ greedy. Expected SARSA is SARSA averaged over $A'$.
  • Cliff walking (lecture 18): Q-learning learns the shortest route hugging the cliff (13 steps) but, because it keeps exploring, falls often during training (about $-50$ average return). SARSA learns a longer, safer route (about 17 steps) and gets a better training return (about $-25$). If $\varepsilon$ decays, both reach the optimal route. Large $\alpha$ hurts SARSA most; Expected SARSA is stable across $\alpha$.
  • Maximization bias: $\mathbb E[\max_a\hat q(a)]\ge\max_a\mathbb E[\hat q(a)]$. Using the same noisy estimates to select and to evaluate the best action overestimates (positive bias). Q-learning suffers; SARSA much less.
  • Double Q-learning: keep $Q_1,Q_2$. Flip a fair coin; to update $Q_1$: $Q_1(S,A)\leftarrow Q_1(S,A)+\alpha[R+\gamma Q_2(S',\arg\max_aQ_1(S',a))-Q_1(S,A)]$; otherwise swap roles. Behave $\varepsilon$-greedily w.r.t. $Q_1+Q_2$. Memory doubles, per-step cost about the same.
Method — step by step (same episode, three targets, recycling-robot table method)
  1. Parse the episode into a transition table: # | $(S,A)$ | $r$ | $S'$ | $A'$ (next action in the episode, "terminal" if the episode ends). Do this once; it is reused for all three algorithms.
  2. List the distinct $(S,A)$ pairs visited and set the initial $Q$ (e.g. 0.5 for every pair, including pairs never visited).
  3. Process the rows strictly in time order. Keep one running $Q$ table per algorithm; cross out and rewrite after every update.
  4. For each row write: target, then $\delta=\text{target}-Q$, then $\alpha\delta$, then new $Q$.
  5. SARSA target: $r+\gamma Q(S',A')$ using the current table. Expected SARSA: $r+\gamma\sum_b\pi(b\mid S')Q(S',b)$ with the policy given in the question. Q-learning: $r+\gamma\max_bQ(S',b)$ over all actions available at $S'$.
  6. Terminal row: target is just $r$ (the next-state term is 0).
  7. A pair visited twice is updated twice; the second update starts from the already-updated value. Later rows see earlier updates.
  8. Finish with a summary table pair vs algorithm, and one line on why values differ (only rows with a non-greedy $A'$ or a spread-out expectation differ).

  • Optimism of Q-learning in Tutorial 3 Q9: its update is the largest because the max is larger than the sampled value; it is "optimistic" and, in cliff walking, "dangerous".

n-step methods

Asked in: Tutorial 3 Q6 (2-step target) Final 2025 Q3 variations ("n-step ... be ready to state the update") Lecture 19 (n-step return, truncation) Lecture 20 (Ex 7.1, error reduction, random walk) Lecture 21 (Ex 7.4, off-policy n-step)

Know this cold
  • n-step return: $G_{t:t+n}=R_{t+1}+\gamma R_{t+2}+\dots+\gamma^{n-1}R_{t+n}+\gamma^nV_{t+n-1}(S_{t+n})$. $n=1$ is TD(0); $n\ge T-t$ is Monte Carlo.
  • Truncation: if $t+n\ge T$ the episode ended inside the window; $G_{t:t+n}=G_t$, the ordinary MC return, with no bootstrap term (since $V(S_T)=0$). No artificial rewards are added.
  • n-step TD update (made at time $t+n$, for the state $S_t$): $V(S_t)\leftarrow V(S_t)+\alpha[G_{t:t+n}-V(S_t)]$. The update is delayed by $n$ steps; the last $n$ states are updated after termination.
  • n-step TD error $\delta_{t:t+n}=G_{t:t+n}-V(S_t)$. With $V$ fixed: $\delta_{t:t+n}=\sum_{k=t}^{t+n-1}\gamma^{k-t}\delta_k$ (telescoping; Ex 7.1).
  • n-step Bellman equation: $v_\pi(s)=\mathbb E_\pi[\sum_{k=1}^n\gamma^{k-1}R_{t+k}+\gamma^nv_\pi(S_{t+n})\mid S_t=s]$.
  • Error reduction: $\max_s|\mathbb E[G_{t:t+n}\mid s]-v_\pi(s)|\le\gamma^n\max_s|V(s)-v_\pi(s)|$. One-step contracts by $\gamma$, n-step by $\gamma^n$ (e.g. $\gamma=0.5,n=3$: $0.125$; $\gamma=0.9,n=10$: $0.3487$). Cost: more reward noise (variance) and longer delay.
  • n-step SARSA: $G_{t:t+n}=\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma^{i-1}R_{t+i}+\gamma^nQ_{t+n-1}(S_{t+n},A_{t+n})$; update $Q(S_t,A_t)\leftarrow Q(S_t,A_t)+\alpha[G_{t:t+n}-Q(S_t,A_t)]$; $A_{t+n}$ chosen by the same on-policy behaviour. $n=1$ is SARSA.
  • n-step SARSA as TD errors (Ex 7.4): $G_{t:t+n}=Q_{t-1}(S_t,A_t)+\sum_{k=t}^{\min(t+n-1,T-1)}\gamma^{k-t}\delta_k$ with $\delta_k=R_{k+1}+\gamma Q_k(S_{k+1},A_{k+1})-Q_{k-1}(S_k,A_k)$. The $\min$ handles termination; the leftover term $\gamma^{T-t}Q(S_T,\cdot)=0$.
  • Off-policy n-step: multiply the whole increment by the ratio. State values: $V(S_t)\leftarrow V(S_t)+\alpha\rho_{t:t+n-1}[G_{t:t+n}-V(S_t)]$ with $\rho_{t:h}=\prod_{k=t}^{\min(h,T-1)}\pi(A_k\mid S_k)/b(A_k\mid S_k)$. Action values: ratio $\rho_{t+1:t+n}$, one index later, because $A_t$ is already given. $\rho=0$ leaves the estimate unchanged.
Method — step by step
  1. Write the episode as $S_0,R_1,S_1,R_2,\dots,R_T,S_T$ and fix $n$, $\gamma$, $\alpha$, $T$.
  2. For each state to update ($t=0,1,\dots$) test: is $t+n\lt T$? If yes include $n$ rewards plus $\gamma^nV(S_{t+n})$. If no, sum all remaining rewards and add nothing.
  3. Discount the rewards by $\gamma^0,\gamma^1,\dots$ (first reward undiscounted) and the bootstrap by $\gamma^n$.
  4. Update $V(S_t)\leftarrow V(S_t)+\alpha(G_{t:t+n}-V(S_t))$. Use the value of $V(S_{t+n})$ as it stands when the update is made (before $S_{t+n}$ itself has been updated; the update of $S_t$ occurs earlier in time than the update of $S_{t+n}$).
  5. When $n$ steps of lookahead do not exist (last $n$ states), the target is the plain return.
  6. n-step SARSA: same, but bootstrap from $Q(S_{t+n},A_{t+n})$ with the action actually selected; update the pair $(S_t,A_t)$ only.
  7. Off-policy: compute the right ratio range, multiply the increment (not the target) by it.
  8. To compare $n$ values, tabulate $G_{t:t+n}$ for $n=1,2,3,\dots$ for one state: it moves from the TD target to the MC return.

Traps and tricky points
  • The bootstrap is discounted by $\gamma^n$ and sits at $S_{t+n}$, not $S_{t+n-1}$. Count carefully: $n=2$ uses $R_{t+1},R_{t+2}$ and $V(S_{t+2})$.
  • Do not add a $\gamma^nV(\cdot)$ term when the episode ends inside the window. The terminal value is zero, and the target becomes the full MC return.
  • $n$-step TD is not the same as "TD($\lambda$)": $n$ is fixed, one return length; $\lambda$ mixes lengths (standard result).
  • The state updated at time $t+n$ is $S_t$, not $S_{t+n}$. Early in the episode (first $n-1$ steps) no update is possible.
  • Equal values after 1-, 2-, 3-step TD in lecture 20 ($0.55$ everywhere) is a special case: zero intermediate rewards, $\gamma=1$ and one terminal reward. With nonzero rewards the three updates differ.
  • The error-reduction bound is about the expected target; sampled n-step returns carry more reward noise, so bigger $n$ is not automatically better.
  • Off-policy ratio multiplies the increment $\alpha\rho[G-V]$, not $G$ alone. Weighted-IS style normalisation is not used here.
  • n-step SARSA ratio starts at $t+1$ (the pair $(S_t,A_t)$ is given); the state-value version starts at $t$. Mixing these is a typical lost mark.
  • The sum-of-TD-errors identity for $V$ needs $V$ fixed. For SARSA the time indices of $Q_{k}$ and $Q_{k-1}$ in $\delta_k$ are exactly what make the telescoping work.
  • With $n=\infty$ (or $n\ge T$) n-step TD is MC; with $n=1$ it is TD(0): say this in any "relate" question.

Function approximation: linear semi-gradient TD(0), features, fixed point

Asked in: Final 2025 Q4 (a) feature matrix and rank, 3 marks Final 2025 Q4 (b) dimension $d$, 2 marks Final 2025 Q4 (c) final $w$ after TD(0), 4 marks Lecture / tutorial emphasis (Oct 26 and Oct 28 lectures: polynomial and Fourier features, TD fixed point, Textbook Exercise 9.3)

Know this cold
  • Linear form: $\hat v(s,w)=w^\top x(s)=\sum_{i=1}^d w_i x_i(s)$, so $\nabla_w\hat v(s,w)=x(s)$ (a vector, same length as $w$). “Linear” means linear in $w$, not in the state: $s_1 s_2$ or $s_1^2$ as features are still linear function approximation.
  • Semi-gradient TD(0): $\delta_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma\,w_t^\top x(S_{t+1})-w_t^\top x(S_t)$, then $w_{t+1}=w_t+\alpha\,\delta_t\,x(S_t)$. Both value estimates use the current $w_t$ (using $w_{t+1}$ on the right would be implicit; the professor corrected himself on exactly this point).
  • Terminal state: $\hat v(\text{terminal},w)=0$ for every $w$. The question has to say which state is terminal; if it does, never evaluate $w^\top x(\text{terminal})$.
  • Feature matrix $X\in\mathbb R^{|\mathcal S|\times d}$: row $i$ is $x(s_i)^\top$, column $j$ is basis function $x_j$ evaluated at all states. $\hat v = Xw$ gives all state values at once. Because $d\ll|\mathcal S|$ in practice, $X$ is tall.
  • Dimension of $w$ = number of columns of $X$ = length of one feature vector. It is not the number of states and not the number of rows.
  • Full column rank means $\operatorname{rank}(X)=d$, i.e. the columns are linearly independent: $\sum_j a_j X_{:j}=0\Rightarrow$ all $a_j=0$. It means no feature is redundant, and $w\mapsto Xw$ is one-to-one, so different $w$ give different value functions. It can only hold if the number of states is at least $d$.
  • Tabular is the special case $X=I$ (one-hot features): $w_i$ is simply $V(s_i)$ and the update touches only the visited state.
  • Gradient Monte Carlo: $w\leftarrow w+\alpha[G_t-\hat v(S_t,w)]\nabla\hat v(S_t,w)$. Target $G_t$ is unbiased, but needs the whole episode. Semi-gradient TD target $R+\gamma\hat v(S',w)$ is biased but fully incremental.
  • Why “semi”: the target also depends on $w$, but it is treated as a constant when differentiating; only $\hat v(S_t,w)$ is differentiated.
  • Mean update and TD fixed point: $w_{t+1}=w_t+\alpha[R_{t+1}x_t-x_t(x_t-\gamma x_{t+1})^\top w_t]$. Define $A=\mathbb E[x_t(x_t-\gamma x_{t+1})^\top]$ ($d\times d$) and $b=\mathbb E[R_{t+1}x_t]$ ($d$-vector). Expected update $=b-Aw$, so $Aw_{TD}=b$ and $w_{TD}=A^{-1}b$. In matrix form (standard result) $A=X^\top D(I-\gamma P)X$, $b=X^\top D\bar r$ with $D=\operatorname{diag}(\mu)$.
  • Objective: $\overline{VE}(w)=\sum_s\mu(s)[v_\pi(s)-\hat v(s,w)]^2$ with $\mu$ the on-policy steady-state distribution. The exact gradient step is not implementable for three reasons: huge state sum, unknown $\mu$, unknown $v_\pi$. Sampling $S_t$ fixes the first two, $G_t$ or the TD target fixes the third.
  • Polynomial features: $x_i(s)=\prod_{j=1}^K s_j^{c_{ij}}$ with each $c_{ij}\in\{0,\dots,n\}$, so there are $(n+1)^K$ features. Here $n$ is the largest allowed exponent per component, not the number of features. $K=2,n=2$ gives 9 (Exercise 9.3); $K=3,n=2$ gives 27; $K=3,n=1$ gives 8.
  • Fourier (cosine) basis: $s\in[0,1]^K$, $x_i(s)=\cos(\pi\,s^\top c_i)$, $c_{ij}\in\{0,\dots,n\}$, again $(n+1)^K$ features; $c_{ij}=0$ means no variation along dimension $j$; bigger $c_{ij}$ means faster oscillation. Useful for periodic structure.
  • Constant feature: without $x_1(s)=1$, the state with all components zero is forced to have $\hat v=0$ for every $w$. A constant feature adds an intercept.
  • Neural-network value function: $\hat v(x,w)=\sum_{k=1}^K w_k\,\sigma\big(\sum_{l=1}^d w_{kl}x_l\big)$ (one hidden layer). $w$ collects all $w_k$ and $w_{kl}$; there is one network, not one per state. The TD update has the same form $w\leftarrow w+\alpha\delta\nabla_w\hat v$, with the gradient obtained by the chain rule through the network. Activation $\sigma$: differentiable, monotonically increasing, bounded; tanh in $(-1,1)$ with $\tanh 0=0$; sigmoid in $(0,1)$ with $\sigma(0)=\tfrac12$. ReLU $=\max(0,\xi)$ is unbounded and has no derivative at 0, so it is smoothed (GELU was mentioned) or used in deep networks.
Method — step by step
  1. Write the rule before any number: $w\leftarrow w+\alpha\,[r+\gamma\,w^\top x(s')-w^\top x(s)]\,x(s)$, and note $\hat v(\text{terminal})=0$.
  2. List the trajectory as transitions $(s,r,s')$: a trajectory s1, 2, s3, 4, s2, 1, s1, 0, s4 means $s_1\to s_3$ with reward 2, then $s_3\to s_2$ reward 4, and so on. Rewards sit between the states. With $n$ states listed there are $n-1$ transitions.
  3. Write the feature matrix. Check rank: for a small $X$ try to solve $\sum a_jc_j=0$ by looking at rows that isolate a column (e.g. a row with a single 1), or compute $\det(X^\top X)\neq 0$.
  4. $d=$ number of columns; write down the shape of $w$ and the initial $w$ (usually $0$).
  5. For each transition in time order, make a table: $\delta$ (compute $w^\top x(s)$ and $w^\top x(s')$ from the current $w$), then $\alpha\delta\,x(s)$, then the new $w$ component by component.
  6. Only the components where $x(s)\neq0$ change. Use this as an arithmetic check.
  7. Optional final check: compute $\hat v(s_i)=w^\top x(s_i)$ for all states (that is $Xw$).

Traps and tricky points
  • Updating $\delta$ with a $w$ that was already updated in the same step: compute both $w^\top x(s)$ and $w^\top x(s')$ from $w_t$, then update.
  • Treating the reward as belonging to the state: reward $r$ in s1, 2, s3 is received on the transition $s_1\to s_3$, and it is used when updating $s_1$.
  • Forgetting that states share weights when features overlap: in Final 2025 the step at $s_1$ changes $w_1$ and $w_3$, which also changes the predicted values of $s_3$ and $s_4$ although they were not visited.
  • Writing $d=4$ because there are four states. The rank bound is $\operatorname{rank}\le\min(|\mathcal S|,d)$.
  • Rank-deficient $X$: the weight vector is not unique (predictions may still be), $A$ may be singular, and the fixed point formula needs invertibility.
  • Mixing up the terminal flag: a final transition into a terminal state has target just $r$.
  • Confusing $n$ (maximum exponent) with the number of features, as a student did in class. Also note the lecture’s “order $n$” bounds each component’s exponent, so total degree can reach $Kn$ (unlike the usual total-degree convention).
  • Fourier features need $s_j\in[0,1]$; the cosine argument is $\pi\,s^\top c_i$.
  • The TD fixed point is not the minimiser of $\overline{VE}$ in general (standard result). Positive recurrence or irreducibility of the chain is what makes $\mu(s)\gt 0$ for all states.
  • “Positive definite” for the non-symmetric $A$ means all eigenvalues have positive real part (positive stable) in the lecture’s sense.
  • Gradient-descent factors: the $2$ from differentiating the square and the minus sign from $-\hat v$ are absorbed (step size $\alpha/2$) so the final update has $+\alpha[\dots]\nabla\hat v$, with no leftover 2.

Policy gradient: score functions, REINFORCE, baseline, actor–critic

Asked in: Final 2025 Q5 (a) $\nabla_{\theta_\mu}\ln\pi$, 4 marks Final 2025 Q5 (b) $\nabla_{\theta_\sigma}\ln\pi$, 4 marks Lecture / tutorial emphasis (Nov 11 lecture; Nov 20 tutorial: Problem A Gaussian, Problem B zero-mean score, sigmoid policy, numerical actor–critic step)

Know this cold
  • Log-derivative identity: $\nabla\ln\pi(a\mid s,\theta)=\dfrac{\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\theta)}{\pi(a\mid s,\theta)}$. It is the building block of every update below. $\nabla_\theta\ln\pi$ is called the score function; it has the same dimension as $\theta$.
  • Policy gradient theorem (episodic): $\nabla J(\theta)\propto\sum_s\mu(s)\sum_a q_\pi(s,a)\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\theta)=\mathbb E_\pi[q_\pi(S,A)\nabla\ln\pi(A\mid S,\theta)]$. The gradient never falls on $q_\pi$ or on $\mu$. The proportionality constant ($\sum_{s'}\eta(s')$) must be positive, otherwise the direction of ascent would flip. The update is gradient ascent on $J$ when $J$ is a value to maximise (for a loss, descent).
  • REINFORCE: $\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha\,G_t\,\nabla\ln\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\theta)$. Monte Carlo: needs the full return, so only after the episode. (For $\gamma\lt1$ the textbook includes a factor $\gamma^t$; the lecture proof assumed $\gamma=1$ and noted the factor is often dropped.)
  • With baseline: $\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha\,(G_t-b(S_t))\,\nabla\ln\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\theta)$. Condition: $b$ must not depend on the action $A_t$ (it may depend on the state, or be a constant). Purpose: variance reduction (not guaranteed for every baseline). Common choice $b(s)=\hat v(s,w)$, giving an advantage estimate.
  • Why a baseline is unbiased: $\sum_a b(s)\nabla\pi(a\mid s)=b(s)\nabla\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)=b(s)\nabla(1)=0$. Equivalent statement: $\mathbb E_\pi[\nabla\ln\pi]=\sum_a\nabla\pi=\nabla 1=\mathbf 0$ (Problem B).
  • One-step actor–critic: $\delta_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma\hat v(S_{t+1},w)-\hat v(S_t,w)$; actor $\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha\,\delta_t\,\nabla_\theta\ln\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\theta)$; critic $w\leftarrow w+\beta\,\delta_t\,\nabla_w\hat v(S_t,w)$ (for linear $\hat v$: $\nabla_w\hat v=x(S_t)$). One TD error, two updates, computed once per step.
  • Step sizes: $\alpha\ll\beta$ (actor slow, critic fast; lecture example $\beta=0.5$, $\alpha\approx0.0005$). Reason: $v_{\pi_\theta}$ moves whenever $\theta$ moves, so the critic must see the policy as almost stationary. Conditions: $\sum\alpha_t=\sum\beta_t=\infty$, $\sum\alpha_t^2,\sum\beta_t^2\lt\infty$, $\alpha_t/\beta_t\to0$; example $\alpha_t=1/(t+1)$, $\beta_t=1/(t+1)^\kappa$, $\kappa\in(0.5,1)$.
  • Assumptions on the policy class: $\pi(a\mid s,\theta)\gt0$ for all actions, differentiable in $\theta$, finite gradient.
  • Score functions to know:
    • Gaussian, $\mu=\theta_\mu^\top x_\mu(s)$, $\sigma=\exp(\theta_\sigma^\top x_\sigma(s))$: $\nabla_{\theta_\mu}\ln\pi=\dfrac{a-\mu}{\sigma^2}x_\mu(s)$ and $\nabla_{\theta_\sigma}\ln\pi=\Big[\dfrac{(a-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2}-1\Big]x_\sigma(s)$. For a general link the second is $\Big[\dfrac{(a-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2}-1\Big]\dfrac{\nabla_{\theta_\sigma}\sigma}{\sigma}$ (the form the TA derived in class).
    • Softmax with preferences $h=\theta^\top x(s,a)$: $\nabla\ln\pi(a\mid s)=x(s,a)-\sum_b\pi(b\mid s)x(s,b)$. At $\theta=0$ with $n$ actions every action has probability $1/n$.
    • Sigmoid, two actions, $\pi(A_1)=\sigma(\theta)$: $\nabla\ln\pi(A_1)=1-\sigma(\theta)$, $\nabla\ln\pi(A_2)=-\sigma(\theta)$ (with a feature vector, multiply by $x$). At $\theta=0$ both probabilities are $\tfrac12$.
    • Multivariate Gaussian with covariance $\Sigma$ (standard result, in the paper’s variations list): $\nabla_\mu\ln\pi=\Sigma^{-1}(a-\mu)$.
  • Why policy gradient: short corridor with state-independent features $x(s,\text{right})=(1,0)$, $x(s,\text{left})=(0,1)$. $\varepsilon$-greedy value methods cannot represent the best mixture (about 0.59 right); with $\varepsilon=0.1$ the lecture values were roughly $-44$ (greedy right) and $-82$ (greedy left) against about $-11.6$ at the optimum.
Method — step by step
  1. “Show that” score-function derivation (Final 2025 Q5 template). Step 0: take the log first so products become sums: $\ln\pi=-\ln\sigma-\tfrac12\ln(2\pi)-\dfrac{(a-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$.
  2. Decide which terms depend on the parameter you differentiate by. For $\theta_\mu$: only the last term (since $\sigma$ depends on $\theta_\sigma$ only). For $\theta_\sigma$: the first and last terms ($\mu$ is a constant).
  3. Differentiate term by term, writing the small facts you use: $\nabla_{\theta_\mu}\mu=x_\mu(s)$; $\ln\sigma=\theta_\sigma^\top x_\sigma\Rightarrow\nabla_{\theta_\sigma}\ln\sigma=x_\sigma(s)$.
  4. Part (a): $\nabla(a-\mu)^2=2(a-\mu)\cdot(-x_\mu)$, multiply by $-\dfrac1{2\sigma^2}$: the two minus signs cancel, giving $\dfrac{(a-\mu)}{\sigma^2}x_\mu$.
  5. Part (b): term 1 gives $-x_\sigma$; for term 3 write $-\tfrac12(a-\mu)^2e^{-2\theta_\sigma^\top x_\sigma}$, the derivative of the exponent is $-2x_\sigma$, so term 3 gives $+\dfrac{(a-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2}x_\sigma$. Add: $[\tfrac{(a-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2}-1]x_\sigma$. (The second route, differentiating $\pi$ then dividing by $\pi$, must agree.)
  6. Self-check: take the expectation over $a\sim\pi$: $\mathbb E[a-\mu]=0$ and $\mathbb E[(a-\mu)^2]=\sigma^2$, so both scores have mean zero. A wrong sign or a missing $-1$ breaks this immediately. Also check shape: $\nabla_{\theta_\mu}$ has the dimension of $x_\mu$, $\nabla_{\theta_\sigma}$ that of $x_\sigma$.
  7. “Show the score has zero mean”: $\sum_a\pi\nabla\ln\pi=\sum_a\nabla\pi=\nabla\sum_a\pi=\nabla1=0$ (use log-derivative, swap gradient and sum, sum of probabilities is 1).
  8. “Baseline does not change the gradient”: pull $b(s)$ out of $\sum_a$ (allowed only because it does not depend on $a$), then the remaining $\nabla\sum_a\pi=0$.
  9. Numeric actor–critic step: compute $\delta$ once; critic: $w\leftarrow w+\beta\delta\,x(S_t)$; actor: $\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha\delta\,\nabla\ln\pi(A_t\mid S_t)$ with the score evaluated at the action that was actually taken.

Traps and tricky points
  • Sign errors in (a): $\nabla(a-\mu)^2$ carries a minus from $\nabla\mu$; with the leading $-\tfrac1{2\sigma^2}$ the result is positive $\dfrac{a-\mu}{\sigma^2}$. Use the zero-mean test.
  • Writing the score for (b) without the factor $x_\sigma(s)$ (a vector, not a scalar). The TA confirmed $\nabla\mu=x_\mu$ is a vector.
  • Forgetting term 1 ($-\ln\sigma$) when differentiating by $\theta_\sigma$; the answer then lacks the $-1$.
  • Differentiating $\sigma^{-2}$ by hand without exponential form leads to lost factors of 2; using $e^{-2\theta_\sigma^\top x_\sigma}$ keeps it clean.
  • Notation clash: in the tutorial $\mu(s,\theta)$ is the Gaussian mean, while in the theorem $\mu(s)$ is the state distribution; $\sigma$ is both a standard deviation and the sigmoid.
  • Calling the update “descent”: for maximising $J$ it is ascent (a student made this slip). For a loss such as the router admission $J=\mathbb E|Q_n-N|$ use descent.
  • “Baseline must depend on the state” is wrong: it must not depend on the action; it may be constant. And “every baseline reduces variance” is also wrong.
  • Using two different TD errors for actor and critic: it is the same $\delta_t$ and it is computed once with the current $w_t$.
  • Mixing $\alpha$ and $\beta$: $\alpha$ for the actor $\theta$, $\beta$ for the critic $w$. In the tutorial transcript the labels appear swapped in the numerical example (the TA used 0.1 and 0.05, which does not even satisfy $\alpha\ll\beta$); the example only shows mechanics, so follow the question’s own labels.
  • Evaluating the score at the wrong action: the actor update uses $\nabla\ln\pi(A_t\mid S_t)$ for the action actually taken, not the greedy one.
  • For $\gamma\lt1$ the textbook adds $\gamma^t$ in REINFORCE (and $I=\gamma^t$ in actor–critic). The professor’s derivation used $\gamma=1$; state your assumption if the question has $\gamma\lt1$.
  • Policy-gradient theorem: the TA does not expect the full unrolling proof; he expects short derivations reusing tricks (zero term, swap gradient and sum, pull a baseline out of the sum). Know the five-step outline anyway: product rule, gradient of $q$ only hits $v(s')$, unroll to the terminal state ($\nabla v=0$), collect $\eta(x)$, normalise to $\mu(x)$.

DQN and DRQN: target, loss, replay, target network

Asked in: Lecture / tutorial emphasis (Nov 2 and Nov 4 lectures, Nov 20 tutorial §10; no sample paper asks it yet; the Nov 4 quiz on Chapters 7 and 9 explicitly excluded DQN, so a written question on it is plausible)

Know this cold
  • DQN is Q-learning with a neural-network (deep convolutional) approximator $Q(s,a;\theta)$, plus two innovations: experience replay and a periodically updated target network. It approximates $Q^*$, uses $\varepsilon$-greedy behaviour, and was tested on 49 Atari 2600 games with one architecture and one set of hyper-parameters.
  • Target: $y_j=r_j$ if the episode terminates at step $j+1$ (terminal flag), otherwise $y_j=r_j+\gamma\max_{a'}\hat Q(\phi_{j+1},a';\theta^-)$. The max is over actions of the target network $\theta^-$, at the next state.
  • Loss: $L_i(\theta_i)=\mathbb E_{(s,a,r,s')\sim U(D)}\big[(r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a';\theta_i^-)-Q(s,a;\theta_i))^2\big]$, an expectation over tuples drawn uniformly from the replay buffer $D$.
  • Gradient: only $Q(s,a;\theta_i)$ depends on $\theta_i$; the target term uses $\theta_i^-$, so its gradient with respect to $\theta_i$ is zero (the “semi-gradient” idea again). Exact step: $\theta_{i+1}=\theta_i+\alpha\,\mathbb E[2(\text{target}-Q)\nabla Q]$; the minus from $-Q$ and the descent sign combine to a plus. The expectation is not computable, so use SGD on a sampled tuple and absorb the 2 into $\alpha$: $$\theta_{i+1}=\theta_i+\alpha\big[r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a';\theta_i^-)-Q(s,a;\theta_i)\big]\nabla_{\theta_i}Q(s,a;\theta_i).$$
  • Replay buffer $D$ with capacity $N$: store $e_t=(s_t,a_t,r_{t+1},s_{t+1})$, keep only the most recent $N$, update from a tuple (or mini-batch) sampled uniformly at random. Purpose: break correlation between consecutive observations. The probability that the newest of $T$ stored tuples is picked is only $1/T$. A tuple can be used more than once; only really-realised tuples are stored.
  • Target network: $\theta^-$ is frozen for $C$ iterations (the tutorial calls the period $N$), then reset to the current $\theta$: $\theta_i^-=\theta_0$ for $0\le i\le C-1$, $\theta_C$ for $C\le i\le2C-1$, and so on. The online $\theta$ is updated every step; $\theta^-$ lags the current $\theta$ by between $0$ and $C-1$ updates.
  • Three reasons plain Q-learning with a network is unstable: (1) successive observations are correlated; (2) a small change in $Q$ can flip the greedy action and so change the policy and data drastically (oscillation); (3) the target uses the same $Q$ that is being updated, so the target moves (correlation between action values and targets). Replay fixes (1); the target network fixes (3).
  • Error clipping: the term $r+\gamma\max Q^--Q$ is clipped to $[-1,1]$ (above $+1$ becomes $+1$, below $-1$ becomes $-1$, otherwise unchanged) because it can become very large.
  • Setting: the agent sees only the current screen, so the task is partially observed; the history $s_t=(x_1,a_1,\dots,a_{t-1},x_t)$ (pre-processed as $\phi(s_t)$) is used as the state. Episodic; $\gamma=0.99$. Return $G_t=\sum_{t'=t}^{T-1}\gamma^{t'-t}R_{t'+1}$.
  • DRQN: the algorithm is the same as DQN, only the architecture changes to a recurrent network (feedback loops). On standard Atari it is similar to DQN; trained on normal games and tested on flickering ones (screen fully obscured with probability $1-p$) it beats DQN. The DRQN paper is optional reading; the DQN paper is to be read carefully.
Method — step by step
  1. One DQN update for a sampled tuple $(s,a,r,s')$: check whether $s'$ is terminal. If yes, $y=r$. If not, $y=r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a';\theta^-)$ using the target network values.
  2. Compute the error $\delta=y-Q(s,a;\theta)$ with the online network. If the question mentions clipping, replace $\delta$ by $\operatorname{clip}(\delta,-1,1)$.
  3. Update $\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha\,\delta\,\nabla_\theta Q(s,a;\theta)$. For a linear $Q=\theta^\top x(s,a)$ the gradient is $x(s,a)$; for a table it is the one-hot vector.
  4. Do not change $\theta^-$ at this step; only after every $C$ updates copy $\theta$ into $\theta^-$.
  5. Target-network timeline questions (Nov 20 example $Q(S,A)=2$, $Q(S,B)=4$, $Q^-(S,A)=3$, $Q^-(S,B)=5$, period 2): $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ are produced from the online table, but both targets use $Q^-_0$ (so $Q^-_1=Q^-_2=Q^-_0$); then $Q^-$ is overwritten by $Q_2$. No numeric result was computed in class; the point is the flow.
  6. “Why does training diverge / oscillate?” Name the causes (correlation, policy flipping, moving target), then the matching fix.
  7. Algorithm listing: initialise $D$ (capacity $N$), $Q$ with random $\theta$, $\hat Q$ with $\theta^-=\theta$; per step: $\varepsilon$-greedy action, execute, store $(\phi_t,a_t,r_t,\phi_{t+1})$, sample a random mini-batch, set $y_j$, gradient step on $(y_j-Q(\phi_j,a_j;\theta))^2$, every $C$ steps $\hat Q\leftarrow Q$.
Question patterns and what to do
If the question says…Do this
“Write the DQN target / loss / update”Give $y_j$ with the terminal case, the squared loss with $\theta^-$ in the target only, and the sampled update $\theta+\alpha[y-Q]\nabla_\theta Q$.
“What is the gradient of the loss; why does the target not contribute?”The target depends on $\theta^-$, a fixed copy, so $\nabla_\theta$(target)$=0$; the sign: $-Q$ gives a plus in the update after descent. Mention the factor 2 absorbed into $\alpha$.
“What problems arise when using a neural network in Q-learning?”Correlated samples, the agent staying in one region of the state space, unstable moving targets (small weight change, large Q change).
“Purpose of experience replay?”Random sampling from stored tuples makes consecutive updates use unrelated transitions, breaking correlation; also reuses data.
“Purpose of the target network / role of $C$?”Holds the target fixed for $C$ updates so the network is not chasing its own moving estimate; copy every $C$ steps so the two networks then solve the same problem again. Larger $C$ gives a more stationary but staler target (inference; the lecture states only the stabilising role).
“Compute the update given $Q$ values, $r$, $\gamma$, $\alpha$”Take the max over target-network values at $s'$; subtract the online $Q(s,a)$; scale by $\alpha$ (and clip if told).
“Why clip the error?”The error can be very large positive or negative; clipping to $[-1,1]$ bounds the update size. (Equivalent to a Huber-type loss gradient: standard result, not stated in lecture.)
“What is the state for Atari? Is it an MDP?”Only the current screen is observed (partially observed); the history of screens and actions is used as the state, preprocessed by $\phi$.
“What does DRQN change?”Same algorithm, recurrent network instead of feed-forward CNN; helps when observation quality varies (flickering screens).
“Training reward collapses / Q-values explode”Diagnose: is replay used? Is the target network held fixed long enough? Are consecutive samples used in order? Is the error clipped? Then give the fix.
Traps and tricky points
  • Taking the max with the online network instead of the target network $\theta^-$, or using $\theta_{i+1}$ inside the target.
  • Forgetting the terminal case: when the episode ends the target is $r$ alone, no bootstrap.
  • Updating on the newest tuple only: DQN stores it first and then samples uniformly; the newest is picked with probability $1/T$.
  • Copying $\theta^-\leftarrow\theta$ every step: then it is not a target network at all. The target network changes only every $C$ steps.
  • Confusing the two $N$s and two $Q$s: replay capacity $N$ versus the tutorial’s update period $N$ (the lectures call it $C$); in the router example $Q_n$ is queue length and $N$ a target queue length.
  • Calling DQN an on-policy method: the update uses $\max$ (Q-learning, off-policy) and data drawn from old policies in the buffer.
  • The lecture transcript says DQN reached above 75% of the human score on all 49 games; the note-taker flagged that the paper itself reports this for fewer games. Safe statements: better than earlier methods on 43 of 49 games, same architecture for every game, Video Pinball highest at about 2539% of the human score.
  • Atari $\gamma=0.99$ (slightly below one for stability) even though the task is episodic.
  • Clipping applies to the error term as taught here; reward clipping is a different trick (standard result, not taught in lecture).
  • Not taught in lectures, so do not rely on them: Double DQN, prioritised replay, dueling networks, frame-skipping details.

How to attack any numerical RL exam question

Asked in: All six sample papers Lecture / tutorial emphasis (Nov 20 tutorial exam guidance: mostly numerical questions, plus short derivations that reuse tricks)

The routine (apply to every numerical question)
  1. State the update rule or equation first, in symbols, before substituting numbers (the marks are for the method). Examples: $V(s)\leftarrow V(s)+\alpha[r+\gamma V(s')-V(s)]$; $w\leftarrow w+\alpha\delta x(s)$; $Q(S,A)\leftarrow Q+\alpha[\text{target}-Q]$.
  2. Write the transition table from the story: $(s,a,r,s')$ plus next action $a'$ for SARSA. Rewards belong to transitions; with $n$ states in a trajectory there are $n-1$ transitions. Check each table row against the question text.
  3. Settle assumptions in writing: which states are terminal and their value (0 for $V$ and for $Q$ of terminal tuples), $\gamma$ (given, or a stated assumption such as $\gamma=1$ for episodic), initial values, and whether “first-visit” or “every-visit”.
  4. Keep a running table of the values being updated. Process transitions strictly in time order, cross out old values, and write target, $\delta$, $\alpha\delta$, new value per row. Later updates use earlier updated values (repeated states!).
  5. Returns: compute backwards $G_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma G_{t+1}$, starting from $G_T=0$. For periodic rewards use a geometric-series closed form.
  6. Iterative methods (value iteration, iterative policy evaluation): update every state from the old vector (synchronous), tabulate $V_k$ for each sweep, and compare the limit with the exact solution of the linear system.
  7. Derivations (“show that”): write the starting identity, apply one trick per line (log first, pull out of the sum, swap gradient and sum, $\sum\pi=1$), end with the boxed result and a sanity statement.
  8. Always do a sanity check (list below) and write one sentence of interpretation if the question says “comment” or “explain”.
Sanity checks that catch errors
  • Bellman residual: plug the computed $V$ back into $V=r_\pi+\gamma P_\pi V$; the residual must be 0. Final 2025 Q2(d): $V(1)=0.5127$, $V(2)=0.3038$ satisfy $V(1)=0.3+0.7V(2)$ and $V(2)=0.15+0.3V(1)$.
  • Probabilities sum to one: every transition row and every policy; $\varepsilon$-greedy probabilities too. Softmax probabilities sum to 1.
  • Zero-mean score: $\mathbb E[\nabla\ln\pi]=0$ catches sign errors in score-function derivations.
  • Bounds (standard result): with rewards between $r_{\min}$ and $r_{\max}$ and $\gamma\lt1$, values lie within $r_{\min}/(1-\gamma)$ and $r_{\max}/(1-\gamma)$. Value iteration from $V_0=0$ with non-negative rewards increases monotonically toward the limit (Final 2025 Q2 table does).
  • Shapes: the update to $w$ has the same length as $w$; the gradient of $\ln\pi$ has the shape of the corresponding $\theta$ part; $X$ is $|\mathcal S|\times d$.
  • Special cases: tabular features reduce linear TD to tabular TD; Expected SARSA with a greedy target policy equals Q-learning; SARSA equals Q-learning when the next action is the greedy one; $\gamma=0$ gives the immediate reward.
  • Only touched components change: in linear TD only components with $x(s)\neq0$ move; in tabular TD only the visited state or pair.
  • Order check: the final value of a repeated state or pair should reflect two updates, not one.

  • No full proof of the policy gradient theorem is expected; short derivations that reuse tricks are: show a term is zero, interchange gradient and summation, pull a baseline out of the sum. Learn the tricks, not the proofs.
