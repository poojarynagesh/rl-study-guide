Policy Gradient Methods

So far we learned action values and derived the policy from them. Here the policy itself is parameterised, $\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)$, and $\boldsymbol\theta$ is moved by gradient ascent on a performance measure $J(\boldsymbol\theta)$. A value function may help (actor–critic) but is not needed to select actions.

  • The log-derivative trick $\nabla\pi/\pi=\nabla\ln\pi$ and the REINFORCE update $\boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta+\alpha\gamma^tG_t\nabla\ln\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta)$.

  • Gaussian policy for continuous actions: $\nabla_{\theta_\mu}\ln\pi=\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma^2}\mathbf x_\mu$ and $\nabla_{\theta_\sigma}\ln\pi=\big(\frac{(a-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2}-1\big)\mathbf x_\sigma$.
  • Expect numerical questions (TD error, one update step, softmax/sigmoid gradients) plus short trick-based derivations (zero-mean score, pulling a baseline out of a sum).

All earlier methods were action-value methods: learn $q$, then act (nearly) greedily. Policy gradient methods learn a parameterised policy that selects actions without consulting a value function. Notation: policy parameter $\boldsymbol\theta\in\mathbb R^{d'}$ (so as not to clash with the value weights $\mathbf w\in\mathbb R^d$), and $\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)=\Pr\{A_t=a\mid S_t=s,\boldsymbol\theta_t=\boldsymbol\theta\}$. We want to maximise a scalar performance measure $J(\boldsymbol\theta)$ by approximate gradient ascent:

$$\boldsymbol\theta_{t+1}=\boldsymbol\theta_t+\alpha\,\widehat{\nabla J(\boldsymbol\theta_t)}\qquad\text{with}\qquad \E\big[\widehat{\nabla J(\boldsymbol\theta_t)}\big]\approx\nabla J(\boldsymbol\theta_t)$$
Policy-gradient ascent (stochastic estimate of the gradient)(13.1)

Methods that also learn a value function for the policy are actor–critic methods: actor = the learned policy, critic = the learned value function (usually $\hat v$).

13.1 Policy approximation and its advantages

The policy may be parameterised in any way, provided $\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)$ is differentiable in $\boldsymbol\theta$ (the gradient exists and is finite). For a discrete, not-too-large action set the usual choice is the soft-max in action preferences:

$$\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\doteq\frac{e^{h(s,a,\boldsymbol\theta)}}{\sum_{b}e^{h(s,b,\boldsymbol\theta)}},\qquad h(s,a,\boldsymbol\theta)=\boldsymbol\theta^\top\mathbf x(s,a)\ \text{(linear)}\ \text{or a neural network}$$
Soft-max over preferences; linear preferences(13.2), (13.3)

The denominator just makes the probabilities sum to 1. Note that the preferences $h$ are not action values — they are not driven toward any particular numbers; they are driven to produce the best policy.

Advantages over ε-greedy on action values

  1. Can approach a deterministic policy. ε-greedy always keeps probability ε of a random action. A soft-max on action values would not help (the values converge to finite, different numbers ⇒ probabilities strictly between 0 and 1, and a temperature schedule is hard to choose). With preferences, if the optimal policy is deterministic the preference of the best action is driven toward $+\infty$ relative to the others.
  2. Can represent any action probabilities — i.e. a genuinely stochastic optimal policy (e.g. bluffing in poker). When function approximation is heavy, the best approximate policy is often stochastic; action-value methods have no natural way to find it.
  3. The policy may be simpler to approximate than $q$ (e.g. Tetris) ⇒ faster learning and a better asymptotic policy.
  4. Prior knowledge about the desired form of the policy can be injected through the parameterisation — often the most important practical reason.
  5. Smoothness (theoretical): with $\boldsymbol\theta$ continuous, action probabilities change smoothly; in ε-greedy a tiny change in estimated values can flip the greedy action and change probabilities abruptly. This is why policy-gradient methods have stronger convergence guarantees.
From the lectures: parameterised policies, $J(\boldsymbol\theta)$ and the admission-control example Nov 4

What is new. Before, $\pi(a\mid s)$ had no parameter; now the action probabilities themselves depend on $\boldsymbol\theta$. The value function may or may not be used when updating $\boldsymbol\theta$ — the professor stressed it is not necessary (it enters only in actor–critic methods).

$J$ need not be a value function. $J:\mathbb R^{d'}\to\mathbb R$ is whatever performance function we want to optimise. Policy-gradient methods maximise a reward-type $J$ (update with $+\alpha\widehat{\nabla J}$); if $J$ is a loss you minimise it, i.e. update with $-\alpha\widehat{\nabla J}$.

Router admission control. A controller decides, for each arriving packet, to admit or drop it. Goals conflict: high throughput means admitting everything, few overflow drops and low delay mean keeping the queue short — you want the middle ground, a queue length near a target $N$ (an empty queue wastes throughput; a full one drops packets and adds delay).

  • Without a value function: $J(\boldsymbol\theta)=\E\big[|Q_n-N|\big]$ (queue length $Q_n$ vs target $N$) — a loss to be minimised. Parameter: $\theta$ = probability of admitting a packet (drop with probability $1-\theta$).
  • With a value function: design rewards (penalty per drop, reward per delivery, larger penalty for larger delay) and maximise the value over the policy parameter.

Assumptions on the policy class (needed for the theory): (1) $\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)>0$ for all $a,s,\boldsymbol\theta$; (2) differentiable in $\boldsymbol\theta$; (3) $\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\pi$ exists and is finite. Softmax satisfies all three: every action has positive probability for any $\boldsymbol\theta$, and at $\boldsymbol\theta=\mathbf 0$ all numerators are $e^0=1$, so with $n$ actions each gets probability $1/n$. A neural network for $h$ works equally well. (A purely greedy deterministic policy violates (1).)

Example 13.1 — short corridor with switched actions

Three non-terminal states in a row, then the goal G; reward $-1$ per step; actions left and right. They behave normally in the first and third states (left in the first state means "stay"), but are reversed in the second state. With features $\mathbf x(s,\text{right})=(1,0)^\top$, $\mathbf x(s,\text{left})=(0,1)^\top$ for all $s$, every state looks identical to the function approximator. An ε-greedy action-value method must therefore pick right w.p. $1-\varepsilon/2$ everywhere or left w.p. $1-\varepsilon/2$ everywhere. With ε = 0.1 these give $J=v(S)\approx-44$ and $\approx-82$. A specific stochastic mix is far better: the optimum is $\Pr(\text{right})\approx0.59$ with $J\approx-11.6$.

Solving the corridor exactly (Exercise 13.1)

Let $p=\Pr(\text{right})$ in every state and $v_1,v_2,v_3$ the values of the three states (start = state 1).

$$v_1=-1+p\,v_2+(1-p)v_1,\qquad v_2=-1+p\,v_1+(1-p)v_3,\qquad v_3=-1+(1-p)v_2$$

Solving: $v_1=v_2-\tfrac1p$ and $v_2\,p(1-p)=-(3-p)$, hence

$$J(p)=v_1=-\frac{2(2-p)}{p(1-p)},\qquad J'(p)=0\iff p^2-4p+2=0\iff p^*=2-\sqrt2\approx0.586,\quad J(p^*)\approx-11.66$$

13.2 The policy gradient theorem

With a continuous parameter, the effect of $\boldsymbol\theta$ on the action selection is smooth. But $J$ also depends on the state distribution, which depends on $\boldsymbol\theta$ in a way that is typically unknown (it depends on the environment). The theorem gives the gradient without the derivative of that distribution. Episodic case, $J(\boldsymbol\theta)\doteq v_{\pi_{\boldsymbol\theta}}(s_0)$ (13.4):

$$\nabla J(\boldsymbol\theta)\ \propto\ \sum_s\mu(s)\sum_aq_\pi(s,a)\,\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)$$
Policy gradient theorem (episodic)(13.5)

$\mu$ is the on-policy distribution under $\pi$ (Ch 9, eq. 9.2); in the episodic case the constant of proportionality is the mean episode length (a positive number). The gradient acts only on $\pi$, never on $q_\pi$ or on $\mu$.

Proof of the policy gradient theorem (episodic case) — unrolling

Write $\nabla$ for $\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}$ and suppress the dependence on $\boldsymbol\theta$. For any $s\in\mathcal S$:

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla v_\pi(s)&=\nabla\Big[\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)q_\pi(s,a)\Big]=\sum_a\Big[\nabla\pi(a\mid s)\,q_\pi(s,a)+\pi(a\mid s)\,\nabla q_\pi(s,a)\Big]\\ &=\sum_a\Big[\nabla\pi(a\mid s)\,q_\pi(s,a)+\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\,\nabla\big(r+v_\pi(s')\big)\Big]\\ &=\sum_a\Big[\nabla\pi(a\mid s)\,q_\pi(s,a)+\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)\,\nabla v_\pi(s')\Big] \end{aligned}$$

The gradient of $q_\pi$ reaches only $v_\pi(s')$, because $p$ and $r$ do not depend on $\boldsymbol\theta$. The result has the same form for $\nabla v_\pi(s')$, so unroll again and again:

$$\nabla v_\pi(s)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal S}\ \underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Pr(s\to x,k,\pi)}_{\eta(x)}\ \sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid x)\,q_\pi(x,a)$$

where $\Pr(s\to x,k,\pi)$ is the probability of going from $s$ to $x$ in $k$ steps under $\pi$ (the recursion ends because $\nabla v_\pi(\text{terminal})=0$). Then, with $s=s_0$:

$$\nabla J(\boldsymbol\theta)=\sum_x\eta(x)\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid x)q_\pi(x,a)=\Big(\sum_{s'}\eta(s')\Big)\sum_x\underbrace{\frac{\eta(x)}{\sum_{s'}\eta(s')}}_{\mu(x)}\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid x)q_\pi(x,a)\ \propto\ \sum_x\mu(x)\sum_aq_\pi(x,a)\nabla\pi(a\mid x)\ \ \blacksquare$$
Why this is the right form
  • It needs $\nabla\pi$ (known, because we chose the functional form) and $q_\pi$ (which can be estimated), but not $\nabla q_\pi$ nor $\partial\mu/\partial\boldsymbol\theta$.
  • It makes sample-based gradient estimation possible: states $S_t$ visited by the agent are already distributed according to $\mu$.
  • Similar theorems hold for the discounted-start and average-reward formulations (13.6 below).
From the lectures: reading the theorem, and why "∝" is allowed Nov 11

Where the proof is used. The professor derived the theorem exactly by the unrolling above (assuming $\gamma=1$, episodic, with a finite action set so the gradient can go inside the sum). The one-line summary of the argument: product rule → the gradient of $q$ only hits $v(s')$ → apply the same relation again and again until the terminal state, where $\nabla v=0$ → collect the "probability of being in $x$ after $k$ steps" terms into $\eta(x)$ → normalise to $\mu(x)$. $\mu(x)$ is the long-run distribution of finding the process in state $x$ and $\sum_{s'}\eta(s')$ is the proportionality factor.

Why it is useful (his words): $q_\pi(s,a)$ can be estimated by running trajectories from $s$, taking $a$ and then following $\pi_{\boldsymbol\theta}$, whereas $\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}q_\pi$ would be hard to estimate; $\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\pi$ is easy because the policy has a known functional form (e.g. softmax). Average-reward versions exist but were not covered in this course.

From the lectures (tutorial): "proportional to" and the dependence of $\mu$ on $\boldsymbol\theta$ Nov 20
  • Gradient ascent, not descent, on $J$ (a student said "descent" first): we move in the direction that improves performance.
  • Why dropping a factor is fine. The missing factor is $\sum_s\eta(s)$ — a sum of non-negative terms, i.e. positive. If $\mathbf g=c\,\hat{\mathbf g}$ with $c>0$, both point the same way, so following $\hat{\mathbf g}$ still improves performance; only the effective step size is rescaled. The condition is positivity, not merely "a constant": with a negative factor $\hat{\mathbf g}$ would point the opposite way and "ascent" would become descent.
  • $\mu$ is the stationary distribution of the current policy. Each $\boldsymbol\theta$ gives a different $\pi_{\boldsymbol\theta}$ and therefore a different $\mu$ — changing $\boldsymbol\theta$ changes $\mu$. The theorem's value is precisely that we never need $\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\mu$; the expectation in the sampled version is over $\mu$ and $\pi$ together.

13.3 REINFORCE: Monte Carlo policy gradient

Because the right-hand side of (13.5) is a weighted sum over states with weights $\mu$, it is an expectation over $S_t\sim\mu$ when following $\pi$:

$$\nabla J(\boldsymbol\theta)\propto\E_\pi\Big[\sum_aq_\pi(S_t,a)\,\nabla\pi(a\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta)\Big]$$
Expectation form(13.6)

Sampling just $S_t$ and keeping the sum over actions gives the all-actions method $\boldsymbol\theta_{t+1}=\boldsymbol\theta_t+\alpha\sum_a\hat q(S_t,a,\mathbf w)\nabla\pi(a\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta)$ (13.7) — it needs a learned $\hat q$. REINFORCE goes one step further and samples the action too, by the multiply-and-divide-by-π trick:

$$\begin{aligned}\nabla J&\propto\E_\pi\Big[\sum_a\pi(a\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta)\,q_\pi(S_t,a)\frac{\nabla\pi(a\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta)}{\pi(a\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta)}\Big]=\E_\pi\Big[q_\pi(S_t,A_t)\frac{\nabla\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta)}{\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta)}\Big]\\&=\E_\pi\Big[G_t\,\frac{\nabla\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta)}{\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta)}\Big]\qquad(\E_\pi[G_t\mid S_t,A_t]=q_\pi(S_t,A_t))\end{aligned}$$

Replace the expectation by the single sample (stochastic gradient ascent) and use $\nabla\pi/\pi=\nabla\ln\pi$:

$$\boldsymbol\theta_{t+1}\doteq\boldsymbol\theta_t+\alpha\,G_t\,\frac{\nabla\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)}{\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)}=\boldsymbol\theta_t+\alpha\,G_t\,\nabla\ln\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)$$
REINFORCE update (as derived; $\gamma=1$)(13.8)
  • Interpretation: each increment is proportional to the return $G_t$ times the vector $\nabla\pi/\pi$, the direction in parameter space that most increases the probability of repeating $A_t$ on future visits to $S_t$. It is large for actions that are rarely chosen (small $\pi$) and that earned a large return — moves toward actions with high return.
  • Unbiased (the expected update is the true gradient direction) and, with small enough α, guaranteed to converge to a local optimum — the theoretical strength of the policy-gradient approach.
  • As a Monte Carlo method it has high variance and learns slowly, and it needs complete episodes (episodic tasks only).
  • With discounting the exact update carries a factor $\gamma^t$ (Exercise 13.2): $\boldsymbol\theta_{t+1}=\boldsymbol\theta_t+\alpha\gamma^tG_t\nabla\ln\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)$ — later steps count for less. Many implementations drop it.
  • Eligibility vector $\nabla\ln\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)$. For softmax with linear preferences (13.3): $\nabla\ln\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathbf x(s,a)-\sum_b\pi(b\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\mathbf x(s,b)$ (13.9) — the feature of the chosen action minus the policy-average feature.

From the lectures: deriving REINFORCE step by step Nov 11

The professor listed what is known and unknown in the theorem: $\nabla\pi$ is known (the form of the policy is our choice); $\mu(x)$ is unknown (a steady-state quantity); $q_\pi$ is unknown (it would need the model $p(s',r\mid s,a)$). Then four moves:

  1. $\mu$ is a distribution ⇒ the sum over states becomes an expectation over the observed $S_t$.
  2. Multiply and divide by $\pi(a\mid S_t)$ ⇒ the sum over actions becomes an expectation over the action actually taken, $A_t\sim\pi(\cdot\mid S_t)$ (the two expectations merge into one over $(S_t,A_t)$).
  3. $q_\pi(S_t,A_t)$ is unknown ⇒ replace by the sampled return $G_t$.
  4. The model is unknown so the expectation cannot be evaluated ⇒ drop it and use the single sample.

Terminology: it is "Monte Carlo" because it uses the full return $G_t$; people often just say "the policy gradient algorithm" or "policy-gradient Monte Carlo". The class derivation assumed $\gamma=1$; the $\gamma^t$ factor of the book's version (and the $I=\gamma^t$ in actor–critic) is the discounted correction and is often dropped in practice.

Why it is inefficient: after every policy change you must generate a fresh trajectory and wait for its complete return before the next update. Tutorial add-on (Nov 20): the two practical problems with the raw policy-gradient equation are (1) $G_t$ needs a complete trajectory, and (2) the expectation cannot be computed because the dynamics are unknown (sampling fixes this; bootstrapping fixes (1)).

From the lectures (tutorial): worked example — a sigmoid policy with two actions Nov 20

Two actions with $\pi(A_1\mid s,\theta)=\sigma(\theta)=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-\theta}}$ and $\pi(A_2\mid s,\theta)=1-\sigma(\theta)$. At $\theta=0$ both have probability ½.

$$\frac{d}{d\theta}\ln\pi(A_1)=\frac{d}{d\theta}\big[-\ln(1+e^{-\theta})\big]=\frac{e^{-\theta}}{1+e^{-\theta}}=1-\sigma(\theta)\qquad\frac{d}{d\theta}\ln\pi(A_2)=-\sigma(\theta)$$

So at $\theta=0$ the score is $+\tfrac12$ for $A_1$ and $-\tfrac12$ for $A_2$. A REINFORCE step after taking $A_1$ with return $G$ is $\Delta\theta=\alpha G\cdot\tfrac12$; after taking $A_2$ it is $-\alpha G\cdot\tfrac12$. (Check the zero-mean property: $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cdot(-\tfrac12)=0$.) E.g. $\alpha=0.1$, $G=4$, $A_1$ taken: $\Delta\theta=0.2$, $\pi(A_1)$ rises from 0.5 to $\sigma(0.2)\approx0.550$.

13.4 REINFORCE with baseline

Generalise the theorem by comparing $q_\pi(s,a)$ to an arbitrary baseline $b(s)$ that does not depend on the action:

$$\nabla J(\boldsymbol\theta)\propto\sum_s\mu(s)\sum_a\big(q_\pi(s,a)-b(s)\big)\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)$$
Policy gradient theorem with baseline(13.10)

It is valid because the extra term vanishes:

$$\sum_ab(s)\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)=b(s)\,\nabla\sum_a\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)=b(s)\,\nabla1=0$$

The corresponding update (sampled exactly as before):

$$\boldsymbol\theta_{t+1}\doteq\boldsymbol\theta_t+\alpha\big(G_t-b(S_t)\big)\frac{\nabla\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)}{\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)}$$
REINFORCE with baseline(13.11)
What a baseline does (and does not do)
  • No bias: the expected update is unchanged for any $b$ that is independent of the action. It may be a constant or any function of the state.
  • Variance: a good baseline can cut variance a lot (not guaranteed for an arbitrary $b$). A natural choice is an estimate of the state value, $b(S_t)=\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)$: then $G_t-\hat v(S_t)$ says whether the return was better or worse than usual from this state, so actions are no longer all pushed up merely because returns are positive (e.g. all rewards positive, or a $-1$-per-step task).
  • Why baselines matter in practice: Figure 13.2 — REINFORCE with a baseline learns much faster on the short corridor.

The baseline can be learned by a Monte Carlo method, since REINFORCE is already Monte Carlo — with a second step size $\alpha^{\mathbf w}$. Book rule of thumb: for a linear $\hat v$, $\alpha^{\mathbf w}=0.1/\E\big[\|\nabla\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)\|^2_\mu\big]$; the policy step $\alpha^{\boldsymbol\theta}$ is harder to set (depends on the range of rewards and the policy parameterisation).

From the lectures: why a baseline changes nothing in expectation, and what it requires Nov 11 · Nov 20

The trick (named in class as one to learn): pull $b(s)$ out of the action sum because it doesn't depend on $a$; swap gradient and sum; $\sum_a\pi=1$ is a constant, so its gradient is zero. In the lecture a student answered the question "what is $\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid s)$?" correctly: zero.

Score function has zero mean (tutorial Problem B). For a fixed state,

$$\E_{A\sim\pi}\big[\nabla\ln\pi(A\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\big]=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\frac{\nabla\pi(a\mid s)}{\pi(a\mid s)}=\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid s)=\nabla\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)=\nabla1=\mathbf 0$$

Hence $\E[b\,\nabla\ln\pi]=b\cdot\mathbf 0=0$ whenever $b$ can be taken out of the expectation over $A$.

  • Condition on $b$: it must be independent of the action. The TA stressed it need not even depend on the state ("terminal state" is not the condition). A baseline like $b(s,a)=\hat q(s,a)$ is not allowed in general — it cannot be pulled out of $\sum_a$.
  • Purpose = variance reduction (not "to get the advantage" — the advantage can be established independently). Warning: not every baseline reduces variance; we hope the chosen one does. The common choice $\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)$ makes $G_t-\hat v(S_t)$ an estimate of the advantage $q_\pi-v_\pi$.
  • Which baseline is best? The professor said the variance-minimising baseline is the value function itself, $b(s)=v_\pi(s)$ (legitimate since it depends only on the state). Strictly the exact minimiser is a $\|\nabla\ln\pi\|^2$-weighted version of $v_\pi$; $v_\pi$ is the standard near-optimal choice. Problem: $v_\pi$ is unknown ⇒ it has to be learned ⇒ actor–critic.

13.5 Actor–critic methods

In REINFORCE with baseline, $\hat v(S_t)$ is a baseline only: it is formed before the action and cannot assess it. In an actor–critic, the value function is also applied to the next state: reward plus discounted next value is the one-step return $G_{t:t+1}$, a bootstrapped estimate of the actual return — a way of assessing the action. That learned value function is the critic. One-step actor–critic is the analogue of TD(0)/Sarsa(0):

$$\boldsymbol\theta_{t+1}=\boldsymbol\theta_t+\alpha\big(G_{t:t+1}-\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)\big)\frac{\nabla\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)}{\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)}=\boldsymbol\theta_t+\alpha\,\delta_t\,\frac{\nabla\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)}{\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)}$$
One-step actor–critic(13.12)–(13.14)
$$\delta_t\doteq R_{t+1}+\gamma\hat v(S_{t+1},\mathbf w)-\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)\qquad\text{(TD error)}$$

The critic is learned with semi-gradient TD(0) (Chapter 9): $\mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w+\alpha^{\mathbf w}\delta\nabla\hat v(S,\mathbf w)$. Everything is fully online and incremental: each transition is processed as it happens and never revisited.

Bias in actor–critic

The one-step return introduces bias into the gradient, in exchange for lower variance and fully online updates. The bias is not because of bootstrapping as such: the actor would still be biased if the critic were learned by Monte Carlo. As with TD, the bias–variance trade-off can be tuned with $n$-step returns or $\lambda$-returns (Ch 7, 12): replace $G_{t:t+1}$ by $G_{t:t+n}$ or $G^\lambda_t$.

From the lectures: actor–critic as REINFORCE with two changes Nov 11

Starting from REINFORCE with baseline, make two replacements: (1) full return $G_t\to$ one-step return $R_{t+1}+\gamma\hat v(S_{t+1},\mathbf w)$; (2) baseline $\to$ the estimate $\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)$. The update can be done as soon as the tuple $(S_t,A_t,R_{t+1},S_{t+1})$ arrives. With $\mathbf w$ frozen the performance cannot be optimal — $\mathbf w$ must also be learned, by TD(0) with function approximation; for a linear $\hat v=\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x(s)$, $\nabla_{\mathbf w}\hat v=\mathbf x(S_t)$.

$$\boldsymbol\theta_{t+1}=\boldsymbol\theta_t+\alpha\,\delta_t\,\nabla\ln\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)\ \text{(actor)},\qquad \mathbf w_{t+1}=\mathbf w_t+\beta\,\delta_t\,\nabla_{\mathbf w}\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w_t)\ \text{(critic)}$$

The same $\delta_t$ appears in both, and both are bootstrapped. (Note the lecture's notation: actor step α, critic step β; the book calls them $\alpha^{\boldsymbol\theta}$ and $\alpha^{\mathbf w}$.) Why "error": the bracket compares the target (reward + discounted next value) with the current estimate $\hat v(S_t)$: "target minus actual".

Versus policy iteration. PI has two nested loops: an outer policy update and, after each, an inner loop that must run until the value of that policy has converged. Actor–critic removes the nesting: both updates run at every step, and the step-size separation $\alpha\ll\beta$ supplies the separation of time-scales that the nesting provided. The overall effect is much the same.

From the lectures: why the actor must be slower than the critic ($\alpha\ll\beta$) Nov 11 · Nov 20

Rule: the two step sizes differ and in general $\alpha\ll\beta$ — e.g. critic $\beta=0.5$, actor $\alpha\approx0.0005$. With time-varying step sizes the conditions are

  1. $\alpha_t,\beta_t>0$;
  2. $\sum_t\alpha_t=\sum_t\beta_t=\infty$;
  3. $\sum_t\alpha_t^2<\infty$, $\sum_t\beta_t^2<\infty$ (1–3: the usual stochastic-approximation conditions);
  4. the distinguishing one: $\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\alpha_t}{\beta_t}=0$, i.e. $\alpha_t=o(\beta_t)$.

Example: $\alpha_t=\frac1{t+1}$, $\beta_t=\frac1{(t+1)^\kappa}$ with $\kappa\in(0.5,1)$ (e.g. 0.7 or 0.8). $\kappa>0.5$ gives $\sum\beta_t^2<\infty$; $\kappa\le1$ gives $\sum\beta_t=\infty$; and $\alpha_t/\beta_t=(t+1)^{\kappa-1}\to0$ needs $\kappa<1$. E.g. with $\kappa=0.7$ and $t+1=10^5$: ratio $=10^{-1.5}\approx0.032$.

Why (intuition from the tutorial). $\hat v(\cdot,\mathbf w)$ tries to approximate $v_{\pi_{\boldsymbol\theta}}$, the value of the current policy. When $\boldsymbol\theta$ changes, the action probabilities change, so the expectation defining $v_\pi$ changes; the best $\mathbf w$ (minimiser of $\tfrac12\E[(v_\pi(S)-\hat v(S,\mathbf w))^2]$) therefore drifts too. If $\mathbf w$ moves much faster than $\boldsymbol\theta$, the critic sees an almost stationary policy and can track its value. Analogies used: a person walking beside a very fast train looks nearly at rest to a passenger (slow actor, fast critic); in cricket, a defensive and an aggressive batting policy give different values for the same match state.

Two-time-scale argument (Nov 11, sketch). Write $\boldsymbol\theta_{t+1}=\boldsymbol\theta_t+\beta_t\frac{\alpha_t}{\beta_t}\delta_t\nabla\ln\pi$: the $\boldsymbol\theta$-increments are $\beta_t$ times something $\to0$, so $\boldsymbol\theta$ is quasi-static, while the $\mathbf w$-increments $\beta_t\delta_t\nabla\hat v$ do not shrink — $\mathbf w$ is the fast variable.

  1. Fast scale: for frozen $\boldsymbol\theta$, TD(0) converges to the TD fixed point $\mathbf w^*_{TD}(\boldsymbol\theta)$ (Ch 9).
  2. Slow scale: substitute $\mathbf w^*_{TD}(\boldsymbol\theta_t)$ into $\delta_t$; then $\boldsymbol\theta$ converges to a local maximum of $v_{\pi_{\boldsymbol\theta}}$.

The tutorial said the full theory is not needed — the intuition above is what to know.

13.6 Policy gradient for continuing problems

For continuing tasks (no episodes) the performance measure is the average reward rate:

$$J(\boldsymbol\theta)\doteq r(\pi)\doteq\lim_{h\to\infty}\frac1h\sum_{t=1}^h\E[R_t\mid S_0,A_{0:t-1}\sim\pi]=\sum_s\mu(s)\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\,r$$
Average-reward objective ($\mu$ = stationary distribution)(13.15), (13.16)

Values $v_\pi,q_\pi$ are defined w.r.t. the differential return $G_t=R_{t+1}-r(\pi)+R_{t+2}-r(\pi)+\dots$ (13.17). The policy gradient theorem keeps the same form (13.5) with "$=$" in place of "$\propto$" (the constant is 1, the proof is in the book). The one-step actor–critic simply replaces $\delta$ by the differential TD error and learns the average reward $\bar R$ too:

Actor–Critic (continuing), for estimating π_θ ≈ π*on-policybootstrapsaverage reward

Initialize R̄ ∈ ℝ, w, θ (e.g., to 0);  initialize S
Loop forever (for each time step):
    A ∼ π(·|S, θ);   take action A, observe S', R
    δ ← R − R̄ + v̂(S', w) − v̂(S, w)
    R̄ ← R̄ + α^R̄ δ
    w ← w + α^w δ ∇v̂(S, w)
    θ ← θ + α^θ δ ∇ln π(A | S, θ)
    S ← S'

Continuing case is brief: the discount factor is replaced by the differential return, there is no $\gamma^t$/$I$ factor, and no episode ever ends.

13.7 Policy parameterisation for continuous actions

With a huge or continuous action space we don't learn a probability per action; we learn the parameters of a probability distribution over actions. E.g. a Gaussian policy: $a\sim\mathcal N(\mu(s,\boldsymbol\theta),\sigma(s,\boldsymbol\theta)^2)$:

$$\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\doteq\frac{1}{\sigma(s,\boldsymbol\theta)\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(a-\mu(s,\boldsymbol\theta))^2}{2\sigma(s,\boldsymbol\theta)^2}\Big)$$
Gaussian policy density (a probability density)(13.19)

Split $\boldsymbol\theta=(\boldsymbol\theta_\mu,\boldsymbol\theta_\sigma)^\top$; the mean is linear in features and the standard deviation is kept positive by an exponential of a linear function:

$$\mu(s,\boldsymbol\theta)\doteq\boldsymbol\theta_\mu^\top\mathbf x_\mu(s),\qquad\sigma(s,\boldsymbol\theta)\doteq\exp\!\big(\boldsymbol\theta_\sigma^\top\mathbf x_\sigma(s)\big)$$
Mean and standard deviation(13.20)

All the machinery of 13.2–13.5 applies unchanged (replace sums over actions by integrals); one only needs the eligibility vector $\nabla\ln\pi$ (Exercise 13.4):

$$\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta_\mu}\ln\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\big(a-\mu\big)\mathbf x_\mu(s),\qquad \nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta_\sigma}\ln\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)=\Big(\frac{(a-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2}-1\Big)\mathbf x_\sigma(s)$$
  • Mean: move $\mu$ toward an action $a$ if it earned better-than-baseline return ($\delta>0$), away if worse; the pull is stronger the further $a$ is from $\mu$ (in units of $\sigma^2$).
  • Std: if the sampled $a$ was farther than one $\sigma$ from $\mu$ ($|a-\mu|>\sigma$) and did well, $\sigma$ grows (explore more); if $a$ was within $\sigma$ and did well, $\sigma$ shrinks (exploit). The policy can thus anneal its own exploration.
  • Another standard case in the exercises: a Bernoulli-logistic unit (two actions, $\Pr(a{=}1)=\mathrm{logistic}(\boldsymbol\theta^\top\mathbf x)$) — the sigmoid policy above.
From the lectures (tutorial): Problem A — Gaussian-policy gradients Nov 20

The TA linked this to question 5 of the previous-year paper. Setup: Gaussian density with mean $\mu(s,\boldsymbol\theta)=\boldsymbol\theta_\mu^\top\mathbf x_\mu(s)$ and std $\sigma(s,\boldsymbol\theta)$; $\boldsymbol\theta$ is the concatenation of $\boldsymbol\theta_\mu$ and $\boldsymbol\theta_\sigma$. Method 1: take the log, then differentiate.

$$\ln\pi=-\ln\sigma-\ln\sqrt{2\pi}-\frac{(a-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$$
  • (a) w.r.t. $\boldsymbol\theta_\mu$: $-\ln\sigma$ and $\ln\sqrt{2\pi}$ have zero gradient; the last term gives $2(a-\mu)\cdot(-\nabla\mu)$ over $2\sigma^2$ with a sign flip from the minus in front ⇒ $\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta_\mu}\ln\pi=\dfrac{a-\mu}{\sigma^2}\,\mathbf x_\mu(s)$. $\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta_\mu}\mu=\mathbf x_\mu(s)$ is a vector, not a scalar.
  • (b) w.r.t. $\boldsymbol\theta_\sigma$: $-\ln\sigma$ gives $-\nabla\sigma/\sigma$; the last term gives $+\frac{(a-\mu)^2}{\sigma^3}\nabla\sigma$ (the 2 from differentiating $\sigma^{-2}$ cancels the 2 in the denominator); factor out $\nabla\sigma$: $\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta_\sigma}\ln\pi=\Big(\dfrac{(a-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2}-1\Big)\dfrac{\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta_\sigma}\sigma}{\sigma}$. With $\sigma=\exp(\boldsymbol\theta_\sigma^\top\mathbf x_\sigma)$, $\nabla\sigma/\sigma=\mathbf x_\sigma$ (the book's form).
  • Method 2 (exercise): differentiate $\pi_{\boldsymbol\theta}$ directly w.r.t. $\boldsymbol\theta_\mu$, then divide by $\pi_{\boldsymbol\theta}$ — the same result.

Numeric check. $\mu=1$, $\sigma=2$, $a=3$: the mean coefficient is $(3-1)/4=0.5$, the std coefficient (before $\nabla\sigma/\sigma$) is $4/4-1=0$ — a sample exactly one $\sigma$ away carries no information about $\sigma$.

13.8 Summary

  • Policy-gradient methods parameterise the policy and do stochastic gradient ascent on $J(\boldsymbol\theta)$. Over action-value methods they can: approach deterministic or stochastic optima, handle continuous actions, change action probabilities smoothly (better convergence guarantees), and embed prior knowledge. Sometimes the policy is simpler than $q$; sometimes not — it depends.
  • The policy gradient theorem gives $\nabla J$ in a form that does not need the derivative of the state distribution.
  • REINFORCE: unbiased, Monte Carlo, high variance, slow, episodic only. A baseline (action-independent) reduces variance without bias.
  • Actor–critic: bootstrapped critic assesses the action; lower variance, online and incremental, but biased.
  • Not covered here: off-policy policy gradient, deterministic policy gradient (Silver et al.), natural policy gradient / TRPO — see the book's bibliographical remarks.

Self-test

Why can a soft-max over action preferences approach a deterministic policy but ε-greedy (or soft-max over action values) cannot?

ε-greedy always keeps ε/|A| probability on non-greedy actions. Soft-max over values converges to probabilities set by the finite differences between true values. Preferences are not tied to any value: if the best action is deterministic, its preference is driven toward infinity relative to the rest.

In the short corridor, why does ε-greedy fail and what is the best policy?

All states look identical under the features, so a value method must choose one mix everywhere (right w.p. 0.95 or left w.p. 0.95 for ε = 0.1, giving $J\approx-44$ or $-82$). A stochastic policy with $\Pr(\text{right})=2-\sqrt2\approx0.586$ gives $J\approx-11.66$.

State the policy gradient theorem and say what is remarkable about it.

$\nabla J\propto\sum_s\mu(s)\sum_aq_\pi(s,a)\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)$. It contains no derivative of the state distribution $\mu$ (which depends on $\boldsymbol\theta$ through the unknown environment) and no $\nabla q_\pi$.

Sketch the proof of the theorem.

$\nabla v_\pi(s)=\sum_a[\nabla\pi\,q_\pi+\pi\nabla q_\pi]$; $\nabla q_\pi$ reaches only $v_\pi(s')$ since $p,r$ don't depend on $\boldsymbol\theta$; unroll to terminal states ($\nabla v=0$); collect $\eta(x)=\sum_k\Pr(s\to x,k)$; normalise to $\mu(x)$, the constant $\sum\eta$ being the proportionality factor.

Derive the REINFORCE update from the theorem.

Sum over states with weight $\mu$ = expectation over $S_t$; multiply and divide by $\pi(a\mid S_t)$ to turn the sum over actions into an expectation over $A_t$; replace $q_\pi(S_t,A_t)$ by $G_t$ (same expectation); drop the expectation: $\boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta+\alpha G_t\nabla\pi(A_t\mid S_t)/\pi(A_t\mid S_t)=\boldsymbol\theta+\alpha G_t\nabla\ln\pi$ (times $\gamma^t$ with discounting).

Why does subtracting a state-dependent baseline not change the expected gradient?

$\sum_ab(s)\nabla\pi(a\mid s)=b(s)\nabla\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)=b(s)\nabla1=0$. The step that needs care: $b$ must be independent of $a$ to leave the sum.

Show that the expected score function is zero.

$\E_A[\nabla\ln\pi(A\mid s)]=\sum_a\pi\,\frac{\nabla\pi}{\pi}=\sum_a\nabla\pi=\nabla\sum_a\pi=\nabla1=\mathbf0$.

Why use a baseline? Does every baseline reduce variance?

To reduce variance (faster, more stable learning), without adding bias. Not every baseline reduces variance — a poor one can increase it. $\hat v(S_t)$ is the standard good choice; $G_t-\hat v(S_t)$ estimates the advantage.

What changes from REINFORCE-with-baseline to one-step actor–critic?

$G_t$ is replaced by the one-step return $R_{t+1}+\gamma\hat v(S_{t+1},\mathbf w)$, so the signal becomes the TD error $\delta_t$. Updates are online after each transition. The critic $\hat v$ is learned by semi-gradient TD(0) with the same $\delta_t$. The price is bias.

Why must the actor's step size be much smaller than the critic's?

The critic estimates $v_{\pi_{\boldsymbol\theta}}$, which moves whenever $\boldsymbol\theta$ moves. With $\alpha\ll\beta$ (i.e. $\alpha_t/\beta_t\to0$) the critic sees a nearly frozen policy and converges to its TD fixed point; the actor then follows the converged critic. This reproduces policy iteration's evaluate-then-improve separation without nested loops.

Compute $\delta_t$ for $R=4$, $\gamma=0.9$, $\hat v(S)=5$, $\hat v(S')=8$, and update a tabular critic with step 0.1.

$\delta=4+7.2-5=6.2$; $w_S\leftarrow5+0.1\times6.2=5.62$.

Give $\nabla\ln\pi$ for a Gaussian policy with $\mu=\boldsymbol\theta_\mu^\top\mathbf x_\mu$, $\sigma=\exp(\boldsymbol\theta_\sigma^\top\mathbf x_\sigma)$.

$\nabla_{\theta_\mu}\ln\pi=\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma^2}\mathbf x_\mu$ and $\nabla_{\theta_\sigma}\ln\pi=\big(\frac{(a-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2}-1\big)\mathbf x_\sigma$.

For softmax with linear preferences, what is $\nabla\ln\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)$?

$\mathbf x(s,a)-\sum_b\pi(b\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\mathbf x(s,b)$: the chosen action's features minus the policy-weighted average of all actions' features.
